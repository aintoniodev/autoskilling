# Hand hygiene kiosk pilot

**Reward independently observed hand hygiene.** Each ward can earn one hospital-funded team meal, offered across day, night and weekend shifts. The infection prevention lead calculates each ward’s baseline from its three most recent monthly audits. Wards below the hospital’s existing compliance target earn the meal by improving five percentage points or reaching the target, whichever comes first. Wards already at target earn it by meeting the target again. Use the first routine monthly audit in each ward’s reward window and the hospital’s existing rules for audit sample adequacy. The nursing director announces the meal and each ward’s target at launch, then thanks qualifying teams in person during existing huddles. The meal is open to everyone on the ward.

**Make the kiosk easy to use.** Before launch, ask nurses from all shifts to try it during protected paid time. Fix placement, access and duplicate entry problems before adding the reward. Tell staff what changed based on their feedback. Make the kiosk the first option in the existing logging workflow on all three wards before the reward starts. Keep the approved fallback available for outages and urgent care. A missed entry carries no penalty, and nurses can resume at the next appropriate opportunity.

**Use two existing data sources.** The monthly observer audit supplies the reward measure: compliant opportunities divided by all opportunities observed under the existing audit protocol. The kiosk supplies the adoption measure: the percentage of scheduled ward shifts with at least one valid, completed kiosk record. Use existing ward and timestamp fields and the ward shift schedule. Count each shift once and exclude test, duplicate and voided records. A shift with fallback records only stays in the denominator and counts as a gap in kiosk use. Label the measure *kiosk coverage by shift* so staff can distinguish it from hand hygiene compliance.

**Give nurses immediate feedback.** The kiosk confirms each saved record. If it already supports a private view, nurses can check their own entries there. The ward’s existing huddle board shows weekly kiosk coverage, the latest monthly audit result and progress toward the meal. Managers receive ward totals with no individual drill-down. Pilot data stays out of performance reviews.

**Stagger the reward and plan the exit.** Give all three wards four weeks with the same fixes and feedback. Then draw the ward order and run one four-week reward window per ward, aligned to the routine monthly audits. Compare each ward with its own baseline and use wards still waiting as a rough comparison. Keep the kiosk default and feedback after each reward window closes. Four weeks later, use **kiosk coverage by shift** to decide what to do on each ward: keep the rollout at 90% or above, fix the workflow and recheck at 70–89%, and drop the current kiosk rollout below 70%. The infection prevention lead reviews the routine audit alongside that decision and can suspend the rollout if compliance deteriorates.

**Check the ways this could fail.** Before launch, the pilot owner tests a duplicate, a voided entry, a fallback entry and a record around a shift change. The ward informatics nurse fixes counting errors through the existing support route. During the existing monthly safety huddle, the infection prevention lead reviews a small sample of ward-level records and any downtime or reporting concerns. Pause the award decision if audited compliance falls five percentage points below baseline or if the audit sample is inadequate. Keep the observer schedule and sampling rules unchanged during the pilot. A single entry can make a shift count as covered, and monthly audits can vary with who and what observers see. Use the two measures together when interpreting the pilot. The pilot owner updates one ward-level report each week and keeps administration under an hour.
