## One working day for code review

**Fix the queue first.** In week 1, ask the 25 engineers what makes reviews wait, then check a sample of late PRs against what they report. Give each ready PR a named reviewer and a backup, and put the oldest unanswered PRs at the top of the team’s existing review queue. Protect a short review slot in the workday and use it to test whether the team has enough capacity. Post the fixes in the team channel in the engineers’ own terms. If the queue still routinely exceeds capacity, adjust the workload before starting the reward.

**Reward the first useful review.** Each ready PR counts once when a reviewer submits an approval after checking the change, requests specific changes, or posts a substantive question the author can act on. Assignment, emoji, “looking now,” and comments that only promise a later review earn no credit. The rule engineers see is “Give the author a useful review within one working day of the PR becoming ready.”

**Measure from ready to first useful review.** Use PR timestamps and a published team working calendar. One working day means eight hours on that calendar, excluding weekends and team holidays. The clock starts when a PR opens ready or moves out of draft. Reassignment and new commits leave the original deadline in place. Each week, report the share of PRs whose deadline fell that week and received a qualifying review on time. Overdue unanswered PRs count as misses. Also show the number of PRs measured, the median alongside today’s 2.6-day figure using a consistent clock, and the age of the oldest unanswered PR. One programme owner samples five credited reviews a week for substance and corrects any miscounts privately.

**Show progress where engineers already work.** After a qualifying review, send the reviewer a private confirmation such as “Reviewed in 5 working hours. On time.” Give reviewers a private target of four on-time responses per five assigned PRs, scaled to the number they actually receive. Someone with two assignments can aim for both, and someone with none has no target that week. A missed review leaves the next PR as a fresh chance. Post the team’s aggregate result and oldest unanswered PR in the existing team channel once a week. Managers see team totals, with individual records available for specific coaching or corrections.

**Run the reward for four weeks.** After the queue fixes are in place, offer one team meal during paid work time if at least 80% of PRs due during the four weeks receive a useful review on time. Confirm that engineers would value the meal before announcing it. Use the four-week aggregate so a bad week can be recovered from. Keep reminders in the working day, with a single nudge to the reviewer and backup when two working hours remain.

**End with the queue in place.** Announce the result at the end of the four weeks and close the reward. Keep reviewer routing, backup coverage, the oldest-first queue, and the protected review slot as team defaults. Measure the same outcomes for another four weeks. If the on-time rate slips or the oldest PRs get older, check where work is waiting and adjust routing or capacity. Budget 45 minutes a week for the sample, corrections, and team update.

**Watch for rushed approvals, cherry-picking, and review overload.** Use the sample to catch rubber stamps and placeholder comments. Keep hard PRs visible by routing them to an owner and reporting the oldest unanswered item. If review work concentrates on a few engineers, rebalance assignments and protect their time. Make the first-response target explicit so engineers can raise a real blocker within a day and still take the time needed to review a complex change.
