## Three-ward hand-hygiene kiosk pilot

**Before launch.** Spend one week testing the kiosk with nurses on day and night shifts on all three wards. Aim for a badge tap, a moment selection and confirmation in under 10 seconds, with ward and time filled in automatically. Place the kiosk on an existing staff route so logging never requires a detour from care or touching a shared screen between cleaning hands and touching a patient. Fix access, placement and sign-in problems before starting the reward clock. Post the fixes in the shift huddle under “You reported it, we fixed it.”

**What earns the reward.** “Clean your hands when required, then record that completed moment once at the next safe break in care.” Each ward independently earns one hospital-funded team meal, with equivalent provision for every shift, if at least 75% of its sampled completed moments are correctly logged during the eight-week pilot. The whole ward shares the reward. Each nurse gets a private starting goal of correctly recording the next eligible moment on each worked shift, then building toward routine logging. Float and part-time nurses use the same goal on the shifts they work. After an absence or a missed entry, start with the next moment.

**How it is measured.** Infection Prevention uses the two scheduled monthly audits to sample at least 24 completed nursing hand-hygiene actions per ward across both months, covering day and night shifts. For this sample, a designated reviewer matches each observed action to one kiosk entry from the same nurse, ward and moment category within 30 minutes, using restricted access and a temporary audit identifier. Each entry can match once. Discard the temporary linkage after the check. A ward with too few observations gets a short makeup sample before the pilot closes. The existing clinical audit continues to count hand-hygiene actions divided by all observed opportunities, including missed actions. Kiosk records measure documentation uptake and stay out of that calculation. [WHO hand-hygiene observation guidance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK144028/)

**What people see.** The kiosk immediately confirms the saved moment and offers a correction option. Nurses can see their own recent entries privately. The existing huddle board shows each ward its own combined result after each audit, such as “9 of 12 sampled moments recorded,” plus one issue fixed that week. Managers get ward totals and access or downtime reports. Individual matching data stays with the designated reviewer. The pilot lead budgets 45 minutes a week for the huddle update, a small check for duplicates or entry bursts, and follow-up on reported barriers.

**How it ends.** Rewards end after eight weeks. Before then, make moment logging the first kiosk action after badge-in and keep the prefilled fields and immediate confirmation. Check documentation uptake at the next monthly audit, about four weeks after rewards end, and review it alongside observed compliance and nurses’ reports of burden. Keep the workflow if uptake holds and care is unaffected. Fix the workflow if uptake falls. Remove the logging step if it adds burden without a clear use for the data.

**What could go wrong.**

- **Logging competes with care or creates a shared-touch problem.** Care and hand hygiene come first. Pause the affected ward’s pilot if nurses report either issue, then fix the placement or input method before restarting.
- **Staff batch entries, duplicate them or log mainly when observers are present.** Keep sampling times unannounced, compare aggregate logging patterns on audited and unaudited shifts, and investigate discrepancies privately. Exclude duplicate and unmatched entries from reward credit. Treat the sampled rate as an estimate.
- **Outages or uneven access skew the result.** Record downtime, replace affected sample windows, and verify that night and float staff can sign in before scoring starts.
- **Kiosk uptake rises while observed hand hygiene falls.** Infection Prevention reviews the decline promptly and pauses the incentive if the workflow may be contributing. The clinical audit remains the safety measure.
