## Three-ward hand-hygiene logging pilot

**Reward reliable logging.** Each ward can earn one hospital-funded team benefit, up to €250, such as a shared break-room improvement chosen by staff across all shifts. A ward qualifies if it meets the logging standard in two of the three pilot months. All three wards can qualify independently. There are no individual prizes, rankings, or rewards for log volume. Nurses log completed hand-hygiene actions at the next safe opportunity. Patient care comes first.

**Measure logging and hygiene separately.** Use the hospital’s existing hand-hygiene definitions and monthly observer audits. During randomly selected audit blocks, observers record each indicated opportunity, whether hand hygiene occurred, and the time and moment category for completed actions. Sample across day, night, and weekend shifts. Keep audit times undisclosed and have observers submit their records before they see kiosk data.

- **Logging standard.** At least 80% of independently observed completed actions have a matching kiosk entry. Match by ward, moment category, and reported time of the action, using a consistent tolerance agreed during setup. Each kiosk entry can match only one action. Report ambiguous matches separately and show how much they could change the result. Store no patient identifiers and use no staff identifiers for scoring.
- **Clinical safeguard.** Track observed hand-hygiene compliance as completed actions divided by all observed opportunities, including missed ones. A drop of 10 percentage points or more from the ward’s baseline triggers an infection-prevention review before the reward is released. Review the sample mix, kiosk workflow, and staff feedback, and pause the logging workflow if it is interfering with care. Staff reports of interference trigger review immediately.
- **Enough evidence to score.** Aim for at least 50 observed completed actions per ward each month, with all shift types represented. Add audit blocks when coverage is low. If the sample remains too small or ambiguous matches could move the result across 80%, mark the month inconclusive and offer one make-up audit. Document kiosk outages and replace affected audit blocks.

**Show staff what they can act on.** The kiosk confirms that the entry was saved and allows correction of accidental duplicates. At the monthly ward huddle, share the ward’s matched count and denominator, observed hygiene compliance and denominator, shift coverage, and progress toward qualification. Share fixes and their owners, such as moving a kiosk or replacing a faulty screen. Keep individual activity and cross-ward rankings off the dashboard.

**Run it for four months.** Use month one to establish the compliance baseline, train all shifts, and test whether observers can reliably match entries. Publish the scoring rules before the three scored months begin. At the end, complete any make-up audits, award qualifying wards, and retire the incentive. Infection prevention decides whether routine kiosk logging is worth keeping based on logging reliability, staff burden, and observed compliance. Continue the existing monthly compliance audits either way.

**Watch for predictable failure modes.** Duplicate or invented entries can inflate raw totals, so only independently matched entries earn credit and the pilot lead reviews suspicious bursts. Staff may log more diligently when they spot an observer, so report the score as logging during sampled periods and avoid claiming it represents every shift. Delayed entries and busy periods can make matching unreliable, so test the match rules across shifts during setup. Finally, a team reward can create peer pressure. Give staff a private feedback route and have managers address workflow barriers without naming or disciplining staff based on pilot scores.
