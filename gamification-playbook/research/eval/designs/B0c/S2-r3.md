# One working day for code review

Run an eight-week team pilot. Aim for **90% of review requests to get a substantive human response within eight working hours**. The current median is 2.6 days. Recalculate the baseline using the pilot’s clock and response definition so the team can compare results directly.

## What is rewarded

Reward the whole team for reliably unblocking authors. If the team meets the final targets, it earns a protected half-day to spend on an engineering improvement of its choice. The engineering manager commits the time before launch and accounts for it in sprint planning. Engineers choose the improvement together.

Each week, two engineers rotate through review duty, with a named backup for absences. The manager reduces their planned delivery work to make room. Duty reviewers answer requests they can handle and find an available specialist for the others. The person on duty owns the handoff until someone accepts it. The team reward depends on response speed and review quality together.

## How it is measured

- **Start the clock** when a ready-for-review PR first requests review from the team. Count eight hours on the team’s published coverage calendar, excluding weekends and team holidays. Reassignment and a return to draft leave the original timestamp intact. Authors can withdraw a request if the work is genuinely paused. Track withdrawals separately and check for patterns.
- **Stop the clock** on a recorded human approval, a specific request for changes, or a concrete question the author must answer before review can proceed. Acknowledgements, bot comments, and promises to review later leave the clock running. A valid response moves the work forward. The final merge can take longer.
- **Report the on-time share** by the week requests arrived, once each request has had a full working day to mature. Open requests past the deadline count as misses. Include request volume, median and 90th-percentile response times, and time to the first approval or request for changes. The last measure shows whether fast first replies are followed by review decisions.
- **Check quality** by having two engineers outside that week’s rotation spot-check five randomly selected responses, or all responses if there are fewer than five. They check whether the response addresses the actual change and whether approvals cover the risks visible in the diff. Record the reason for any failure and correct the metric if a sampled reply was incorrectly counted.

## What people see

Put the live queue where engineers already work. Show each PR’s owner, elapsed working time, and deadline. Alert the duty reviewer when a request reaches six working hours. The weekly team view shows aggregate results, quality checks, and the main reasons requests missed the deadline. Use the retrospective to fix one recurring source of delay. Keep individual rankings and reward scores out of the programme.

## How it runs and ends

Use week one to establish the baseline from the previous four weeks of PRs, validate the measurement with real examples, and agree on coverage. Run the rotation in weeks two through eight. Award the half-day if **at least 90% of requests across the final four weeks are on time, at least 90% of sampled responses pass the quality check, and median time to a review decision stays at or below baseline**. Count the last week after its deadlines mature.

The reward ends with the pilot. At the final retrospective, decide whether to keep the rotation, alerts, or queue based on the results and the workload engineers report. If the team misses the target, identify why and let the pilot expire on schedule.

## What could go wrong

- **Rubber stamps or token questions.** Use the quality sample and time-to-decision measure to expose both. Discuss examples with the team so reviewers share the same standard.
- **A specialist becomes the bottleneck.** Track misses by affected area. Give recurring bottlenecks a backup reviewer and allocate time for them to build familiarity.
- **Review duty becomes invisible overtime.** Check how much time duty reviewers actually spend and adjust sprint commitments or coverage. Keep deadlines within working hours.
- **Large or contentious PRs get deprioritized.** Keep aging requests visible and check misses by PR size. A substantive first response can flag the highest-risk part of a change and explain what needs further review.
