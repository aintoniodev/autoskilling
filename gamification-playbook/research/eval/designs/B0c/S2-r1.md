# One-working-day code review pilot

**Reward.** Run a six-week team pilot. If at least 90% of eligible pull requests receive a substantive first review within one working day across weeks 3–6, and review quality holds, all 25 engineers get a protected half-day for engineering work they choose. Agree with the product lead up front to reserve that time and stagger it for coverage. Keep the reward team-wide. Review counts and response times stay out of individual performance reviews.

**What counts.** A substantive first review is an approval after examining the change, or feedback specific enough for the author to act on. A reviewer can give useful feedback on part of a large PR and say when they’ll finish the rest. Acknowledgments, bot comments, emoji, and reassignments leave the clock running. The review rotation owns routing and coverage. Each day, one engineer and a backup triage the queue, confirm a reviewer, and pick up or reroute requests at risk of going overdue. Budget that duty into sprint capacity.

**Measurement.** Start the clock when a PR is ready and a human review is requested. One working day means eight hours on a published team calendar, for example 09:00–17:00 in the team’s agreed time zone, excluding weekends and team holidays. Count each PR once, using the original request time even if it is reassigned or toggled back to draft. Report the percentage on time for PRs whose deadlines fell that week, including unanswered PRs as misses. Exclude bot-only changes and genuine withdrawals before the deadline, and report withdrawals separately. Track the median and 90th-percentile wait, time to completed first review, and re-review wait. Recalculate the previous four weeks using the same rules before comparing results with the reported 2.6-day median.

**What people see.** Show a shared queue with each PR’s reviewer, deadline, and overdue status. Send one reminder to the reviewer and rotation owner two working hours before the deadline. Share a weekly team summary with the on-time rate, eligible PR count, oldest open requests, and a short note on recurring bottlenecks. At kickoff, show exactly how the numbers and reward are calculated. Use the team’s existing tools and keep the dashboard free of individual rankings.

**Quality and failure modes.** Each week, the engineering lead and a rotating engineer sample five responses marked on time, including larger changes. They check whether the reviewer engaged with the diff and whether any deferred review was completed as promised. Reclassify superficial responses and recalculate the rate. Hold the reward if the final two weekly audits both show recurring shallow reviews or if faster first responses consistently come with slower completed reviews than the baseline. Check the oldest requests for complex work getting sidelined, and check rotation load for interruptions or work spilling outside agreed hours. Adjust coverage or sprint commitments when the queue exceeds capacity. Investigate a rise in withdrawals or draft toggles before accepting the result.

**End.** Use weeks 1–2 to tune routing and verify the measurement, then freeze the scoring rules for weeks 3–6. At the end of week 6, publish the result and award the half-day if the target and quality checks pass. End the reward either way. Keep the queue and rotation if the team finds the workload sustainable, and check the same measures four weeks later to see whether the improvement held.
