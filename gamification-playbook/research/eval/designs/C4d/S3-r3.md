## Three-ward hand-hygiene kiosk pilot

**Reward a safer, easier workflow.** Each ward can earn one hospital-funded team meal, with equivalent provision for every shift, when nurses identify a barrier to logging, test a fix during routine work, and confirm that it makes logging easier without interrupting care. For example, night-shift nurses might test a shorter entry form at a kiosk they already pass. Everyone on the ward shares the reward, including staff who were away. Kiosk entries and audit scores carry no prizes.

**Start with the work.** Give a few nurses from day, night, and weekend shifts 15 minutes of paid time to try the kiosk and explain where it gets in the way. Ask Infection Prevention to approve kiosk placement and cleaning. Put the approved logging screen first, prefill the ward, and offer a quick undo. The instruction at the kiosk is simple: “Clean your hands at the required moment. Log the completed action once, when care allows.” During urgent care or downtime, staff can skip logging and resume with the next case. If nurses still need to make a detour or interrupt care, fix that before starting the reward period.

**Measure adoption and check safety.**

- **Kiosk use.** Use existing kiosk timestamps to calculate the percentage of staffed ward shifts with at least one saved, non-voided entry. Show downtime alongside the figure. This measures how widely the kiosk is used across shifts. It cannot tell you how many hand-hygiene actions took place or how many went unlogged.
- **Clinical safety.** Infection Prevention continues its monthly audits and reports the number of appropriate hand-hygiene actions divided by observed opportunities, with both counts. Use comparable sampling across shifts and days. Observer presence can change behavior, and small samples can swing the rate. Infection Prevention reviews any drop of five percentage points or more from the ward’s baseline before releasing a pending reward. A credible report that the kiosk delayed care or interfered with hand hygiene pauses the kiosk pilot on that ward immediately. [WHO hand-hygiene monitoring guidance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK144028/), [Multicenter study on observation methods](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25111925/).
- **Test the awkward cases.** Before launch, try a double tap, an undo, and an urgent case with no entry. A double tap should save once, an undone entry should drop out of the rollup, and an unlogged case should have no consequence for the nurse. The pilot coordinator handles disputed counts and sends software defects to the kiosk owner.

**Show staff what happened.** The kiosk immediately displays “Entry saved” with an undo option. At the existing weekly huddle, share the ward’s aggregate kiosk-use trend, the latest audit result when available, and one concrete “you reported it, we fixed it” update. Managers see ward totals only. The pilot uses no named rankings, individual quotas, or performance-review data.

**Run it for 20 weeks.** Give all three wards the same setup, paid practice, and feedback first. Use weeks 1–4 as the baseline, then draw the order for four-week reward windows in weeks 5–8, 9–12, and 13–16. Keep the same feedback and kiosk setup throughout, except for safety fixes, which go to every ward immediately. Compare each ward’s change with wards still waiting for their reward window where possible. Three wards give a directional comparison. Use the existing huddle, kiosk rollup, and monthly audit so one coordinator can run the pilot in under an hour a week.

**End the prize after each ward’s window.** Leave the quick-entry screen in place and check kiosk use four weeks later. Set the decision rule up front. The primary number is the share of staffed shifts with a saved entry. For wards below 80% at baseline, keep the workflow if it closes at least a quarter of the gap to 80%. For wards already at 80% or higher, keep it if use stays at or above 80%. Apply the safety check in either case. If use falls short and nurses identify a fixable barrier, make one change and recheck four weeks later. Retire the kiosk workflow if use still falls short or safe use requires extra work that the ward cannot absorb.

**Watch for gaming and burden.** A token entry can make a shift look active, and a busy shift can do excellent hand hygiene with few entries. Keep rewards tied to the tested workflow fix, use the audits to assess hand hygiene, and ask nurses from each shift during the huddle whether queues, shared-touch surfaces, or pressure to log are causing problems.
