## One working day for a useful review

**Start with the review queue.** Ask five engineers, including frequent reviewers and people who often wait for reviews, to walk through recent delays. Give them 30 minutes during work hours. Use what they find to fix routing, name a backup for each review queue, and protect 20 minutes a day for the on-duty reviewers. Tell the team which changes came from their feedback. Put the oldest unanswered review requests first in the queue engineers already use.

**Reward the team for timely, useful reviews.** Each review group earns a team lunch or equivalent meal credit if it answers at least 80% of eligible pull requests within one working day across its four reward weeks. Let engineers choose the reward before launch. Paid review time starts before the reward and continues afterward.

**Count each pull request once.** Start the clock when a human-authored PR is ready for review and first has a review request. Count eight hours on the responsible group’s published working calendar, excluding weekends and holidays. Stop at the first submitted human review that approves after checking the change, requests a specific change, or asks a question the author needs to answer before proceeding. Use existing PR events and review text.

A duplicate request, reassignment, or return to draft never resets the clock. A PR closed or merged after its deadline stays in the denominator. A PR withdrawn before the deadline because the work was abandoned can be excluded, with the reason recorded. Bot reviews and “I’ll look later” don’t count. If an author merges without a qualifying review, that PR counts as unanswered. Before launch, replay these cases against recent PRs and correct the counting rule where needed.

**Keep feedback private.** After a qualifying review, the reviewer gets a private confirmation through the team’s existing workflow. Each engineer can see their own results, with a goal of answering four of their next five assigned PRs on time. For engineers with fewer requests, the next eligible PR is the goal. A missed review or a week away never wipes out progress. The team sees a weekly on-time percentage and PR count in its usual channel. Managers see group totals only, with no individual drill-down or use in performance reviews.

**Stagger the reward and set an end date.** Give everyone two weeks with the queue fixes and feedback first. Use two existing review groups with mostly separate queues and randomly choose which starts its four reward weeks first. The second starts two weeks later. Compare how each group’s on-time rate changes from the shared two-week baseline during the stagger. If the groups frequently review each other’s work or volumes are low, treat that comparison as inconclusive. Give both groups their full reward period, then end prizes on schedule. Keep the queue order, backup coverage, and protected review time as defaults.

**Check for shortcuts and displacement.** Each week, a rotating senior engineer samples five counted reviews across the team for rubber-stamp approvals and vague comments. If two fail, the engineering lead pauses reward payouts, corrects affected counts, and resumes after a fresh sample passes. The rotating engineer also checks for requests posted late, avoidable draft changes, and small PRs getting attention while older or harder ones wait. Anyone can flag a wrong count privately, and the rotating engineer resolves it before payout. Keep the sample, corrections, and weekly update under one hour total.

**Decide whether it stuck.** Four weeks after the last prize period ends, check the percentage of eligible PRs answered within one working day over those four weeks. At **80% or higher**, keep the workflow and end the programme. At **60–79%**, fix the biggest remaining queue bottleneck and recheck in four weeks. Below **60%**, drop the programme and revisit reviewer capacity and routing. Use the same counting rule throughout.
