# One-working-day code reviews

The team earns a shared reward for getting **85% of review requests a substantive response within one working day**. Today’s median is 2.6 days. Calculate the current one-day response rate from the same PR history before launch so the team has a comparable baseline.

## Fix the review queue first

Spend the first two weeks asking engineers who open and review PRs where requests get stuck. Give reviewers protected time during the workday, rotate primary and backup coverage, and put the oldest pending requests first in the existing review queue. Give people paid time to try the workflow and post a short “you reported it, we fixed it” update. Start the reward once reviewers can cover the queue within normal hours.

## What earns credit

Tell the team the rule in one sentence. **A ready PR earns one credit when a requested reviewer submits an approval or a reasoned request for changes within eight hours on the team’s published working calendar.**

Use existing PR timestamps and review submissions. Start the clock when a ready PR first has a requested reviewer. Nights, weekends, and team holidays are outside the clock. The backup picks up requests when the primary is away. A reassignment, return to draft, or new commit leaves the original deadline in place. Count each PR once. A withdrawn review loses credit unless another qualifying review met the original deadline.

Report timely responses as a share of requests whose deadline fell in the reporting period. Unanswered and overdue requests stay in the denominator. Drafts without a ready review request and automated PRs are excluded. A substantive review submitted through the usual workflow counts even if the reviewer picked it up outside the new queue.

## What people see and earn

Use the existing PR notification to confirm a qualifying review promptly and privately. Each reviewer can see their own pending deadlines and whether they covered their assigned reviews or handed them to the backup. That goal scales with the work they receive. After an absence or a missed deadline, the next PR is a fresh chance.

Post the weekly team rate and request count in the engineering channel. Managers see the same aggregate. Individual figures stay private and out of performance reviews. Let the team choose between a paid team lunch and an hour of protected learning time before launch. Award it once if the team hits 85% across its reward window.

## Timing and ending

After the two-week setup, run a four-week reward window. If the team already has two review groups with separate queues, start the second group’s window two weeks later so both groups get four weeks and the team can see what the reward adds to the workflow fixes. With one shared queue, run one window and treat any lift as the combined effect.

Before the reward ends, make the oldest-first queue and backup routing the default. Four weeks after rewards end, use the same one-day response rate to decide what to do. **Keep** the workflow if the rate is at least 85% and quality holds. **Change** coverage or routing if the rate is below 85% but above baseline. **Drop** the incentive if the rate returns to baseline or worse, and keep the workflow fixes engineers found useful.

## What could go wrong

- **Rubber-stamping.** A rotating senior engineer samples five credited reviews a week against the team’s existing review checklist. If two of the latest ten miss a material issue visible at review time or give an unusable change request, the engineering lead pauses rewards and fixes the cause before restarting.
- **Easy PRs crowd out hard ones.** The backup takes the oldest at-risk request and can bring in a specialist. All eligible requests feed the team rate, including the ones nobody picks up.
- **The timer creates pressure or counts a review incorrectly.** Keep review time inside normal hours. The review rotation owner corrects disputed counts privately from the PR history. Budget 45 minutes a week for sampling, corrections, and the team update.
