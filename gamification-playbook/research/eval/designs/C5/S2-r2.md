# One working day for a useful code review

**Fix the queue first.** Before launch, sit down with five engineers who regularly write and review PRs, including people who handle specialist changes. Walk through recent waits and fix the biggest blockers. Give the rotating reviewer a protected 30-minute slot each working day, a named backup for absences, and an oldest-due-first queue in the existing PR tool. If the queue regularly exceeds that capacity, move planned work or add coverage before offering a reward. Show the team what changed: “You told us requests sat with people who were out. They now go to a staffed backup.”

**Reward a useful answer, not a fast click.** Launch message: “If at least 85% of ready PRs get a useful review within one working day during your group’s three-week window, and quality holds, everyone in the group gets a paid half-day for a project of their choice.” Confirm that engineers want the reward before announcing it. The engineering lead thanks the group in person for a specific example of how its reviews unblocked work, then puts the half-day on the calendar. No individual prizes, rankings, badges or streaks.

**Measure it from existing PR events.** Start the clock when a human-authored PR first becomes ready for review, even if routing is late. One working day means eight hours on the review group’s published working calendar; nights, weekends and holidays don’t count. The first substantive review by a non-author counts once per PR: an approval after inspection, a concrete request for changes, or a submitted review that identifies a specific blocker and gives the author a next step. “Looking now,” bot comments, reassignments and chat-only replies don’t count. A chat discussion can count once the reviewer records its conclusion on the PR.

Calculate the **percentage of PRs whose deadline falls in the period that received a qualifying answer by that deadline**. Unanswered, late, and withdrawn-after-intake PRs without a qualifying answer are misses. Drafts and bot PRs never enter. Reassignment, reopening and moving back to draft don’t reset the clock. Before launch, replay duplicates, a withdrawn approval, a Friday request, and a chat-only response. Recalculate the previous four weeks with this rule; the reported 2.6-day median is context, not the baseline for the new percentage.

**Show people what helps them act.** Give reviewers a private queue with due times, an immediate confirmation when their submitted review provisionally counts, and a personal goal based on the requests they actually cover, not a volume quota. A missed review is just a missed review; the next one still counts. Post the team’s weekly numerator, denominator and progress toward 85% in the existing engineering channel. Managers get aggregate results only, with no individual drill-down or use in performance reviews.

**Keep speed from crowding out care.** A rotating senior engineer spot-checks five randomly selected on-time reviews each week using the same rubric before, during and after rewards. Look for rubber-stamps, perfunctory blockers and missed obvious issues. A review invalidated by the check loses credit; if two of the last ten sampled reviews in a group fail, the engineering lead pauses payout and fixes the cause before resuming. Anyone can flag a wrong count privately to the pilot owner, who checks the PR history, corrects the tally and explains the decision. Oldest-due routing helps prevent cherry-picking; use the backup for hard reviews rather than pushing people to approve or work after hours. Budget one owner 45 minutes a week, including the spot-checks.

**Run eight weeks, then take the prize away.** Weeks 1–2: both existing review groups get the workflow fixes, feedback and quality checks. Randomly choose which group gets the reward window in weeks 3–5; the other gets it in weeks 6–8. Compare the change in on-time response rates from each group’s feedback-only period, using the delayed group as a contemporaneous comparison in weeks 3–5. With 25 engineers, cross-group coverage and a changing mix of PRs make this a directional test, not proof.

End the reward after week 8. Keep the staffed rotation, protected time and oldest-due queue as the default. Four weeks later, use the same on-time response rate to decide: **85% or more**, keep the setup without prizes; **70–84%**, fix the remaining bottleneck and recheck; **below 70%**, drop the programme and redesign review coverage. A quality breach overrides a speed win. If the fixes and feedback got the team to target before rewards began, don’t repeat the reward.
