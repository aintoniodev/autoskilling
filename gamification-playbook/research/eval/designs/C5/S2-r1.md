# One working day for a useful code review

**Fix the queue before offering a reward.** Ask six engineers, including frequent authors, overloaded reviewers and people in different time zones, where reviews stall. Give them 30 minutes of paid work time to test the new flow. Put an oldest-first review queue in the team’s existing PR tool, with a rotating reviewer and a named backup for each workstream. Protect a daily review block by taking equivalent sprint work off the duty reviewer’s plate. Publish the first fix in the team channel: “You told us review requests had no clear owner. Every ready PR now has a duty reviewer and a backup.” If the team can already meet the target with those fixes, skip the reward.

**Reward a useful first review, once per PR.** The rule at launch: “A PR counts when a reviewer gives the author a useful answer within eight staffed hours: a considered approval, a specific change request, or a review that explains a concrete blocker and how to resolve it.” An emoji, “looking now,” a handoff, or a bot response does not count. A response is not a merge deadline, and approvals earn no more than change requests.

Use existing PR timestamps: the clock starts when a non-draft PR first has review requested. Count eight hours on the published workstream coverage calendar, excluding nights, weekends and holidays. The denominator is all eligible PRs whose window has closed, including overdue PRs still unanswered. Recalculate the four-week baseline from the same events; the current 2.6-day median doesn’t tell us what percentage met the deadline. Before launch, test duplicates, reviewer reassignment, withdrawn PRs and offline reviews. Duplicates count once; reassignment or reopening does not reset the clock; genuine cancellations before the deadline are logged separately; late withdrawals stay in the denominator. A pairing or chat review counts if the reviewer records its substantive outcome on the PR within the window.

**Make the reward worth taking, not worth gaming.** Confirm with engineers that they want **two paid, meeting-free hours for a technical project of their choice**, then name it at launch. Each workstream can earn it independently if at least **80%** of eligible PRs get a timely review in its three-week reward window and it passes the quality check. Everyone in a qualifying workstream gets the time, including people who were on leave. The engineering lead thanks the group at its retro and puts the time on calendars, with coverage so it doesn’t become evening work.

Reviewers get a private, immediate confirmation when a PR provisionally qualifies. Their personal goal is simply to answer the PRs due on their duty shift; the queue shows what’s left, not a quota or streak. The existing weekly team update shows the team-wide count and percentage against 80%. Managers see the same aggregate, with no individual drill-down or use in performance reviews. Missed a shift or a deadline? Pick up the next due PR.

**Run it for eight weeks, then stop paying.** Give everyone the queue, protected time and feedback in weeks 1–2. If there are two existing workstreams with mostly separate queues, randomly pick which gets the reward window in weeks 3–5; the other gets the same offer in weeks 6–8. Compare the first window with the still-unrewarded workstream and each stream’s fixes-only baseline. Treat the difference as directional: 25 people, shared reviewers and changing PR mix limit what it can prove. If the queues aren’t meaningfully separate, use one shared window and don’t claim to have isolated the reward effect.

**Protect quality and decide what stays.** From baseline through four reward-free weeks, a rotating senior engineer uses the same rubric to spot-check five PRs a week, sampling across on-time and overdue responses and including large PRs. Two inadequate reviews in the latest ten sampled pauses payout for the affected workstream pending investigation. The engineering lead owns the pause; the queue owner corrects miscounts from PR links submitted privately. Check for rubber-stamping, easy-PR cherry-picking and delayed review requests. Keep oldest-first routing and use backup coverage on hard PRs rather than lowering the bar. Budget 45 minutes a week for the audit, exceptions and update.

Four weeks after prizes end, use the **percentage of eligible PRs answered within eight staffed hours** to decide: **80% or more**, keep the workflow without prizes; **65–79%**, fix the biggest remaining bottleneck and recheck; **below 65%**, drop the pilot as designed and revisit review capacity. A quality breach blocks declaring success at any speed. The prize expires either way.
