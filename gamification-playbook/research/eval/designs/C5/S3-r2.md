# Clean hands first: a three-ward kiosk pilot

**Reward accurate logging, not more logs.** Each ward can earn a catered meal for every shift and an in-person thank-you from the chief nursing officer. Announce the reward at launch: “Your ward earns the meal if at least 8 in 10 sampled hand-hygiene actions are correctly logged by the final audit, and independently observed hand hygiene holds steady.” All three wards can earn it. No individual prizes, rankings or streaks.

### Fix the workflow before offering the reward

Ask nurses from day, night and weekend shifts to show where logging interrupts care. Give them 15 minutes of protected, paid time to try the kiosk. Move it if reaching it means leaving care, open directly to the ward’s logging screen, and remove duplicate paperwork if the kiosk replaces it. Keep a short “You reported it, we fixed it” list in the shift huddle. The rule on every shift: **perform hand hygiene when indicated; log only when safe. Never delay care or invent an entry to catch up.**

### Verify what counts

Use the existing monthly observation rounds. In a prespecified sample across days, nights and weekends, the observer tallies a success only when they directly see hand hygiene performed and can confirm that the kiosk accepted one accurate entry for that same action. Divide successes by sampled completed actions. One action counts once, even if several indications apply. A duplicate earns nothing extra; an undone entry does not count; a completed action recorded only on paper still counts as hygiene, not kiosk adoption. Entries outside the sample earn no credit and are not presumed false.

Dry-run this before launch. If observers cannot confirm both steps reliably across shifts without following nurses, collecting identifiers or interrupting care, **do not launch the logging reward**. Instead, give the same team recognition for removing a documented logging barrier and having nurses from day and night shifts verify that the fix works.

Keep the clinical measure separate: **observed hand-hygiene actions divided by all observed opportunities**, including misses, using the [WHO observation method](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/integrated-health-services-%28ihs%29/hand-hygiene/monitoring/9789241598606_eng.pdf). Compare each ward with its own baseline on a similar mix of shifts. Hold the award if either final sample has fewer than 30 usable observations, a shift group is missing, or observed compliance drops by **5 percentage points or more**. These are pilot rules, not clinical standards. Infection Prevention decides whether to pause or resume; the kiosk owner corrects counting faults. Any credible report that logging delayed care triggers an immediate pause.

### Show just enough

After submission, the kiosk says which moment was saved and offers Undo. At huddles, show the ward’s two monthly figures separately: “Correctly logged: 24 of 30 sampled actions” and “Observed hygiene: 43 of 48 opportunities.” Mark small or incomplete samples as insufficient. Managers see ward totals only. No nurse or patient identifiers in pilot tallies, no individual drill-down, and no use in performance reviews. A missed shift has no penalty; the next safe opportunity is a fresh start.

### Stagger the reward, then stop it

Give all wards the fixes and feedback in weeks 1–4 and take a baseline audit. Randomly assign ward start dates in weeks 5, 9 and 13. Each gets eight reward weeks, aligned with two monthly audits. Compare each ward with its baseline and with wards still on feedback alone. With three wards, treat the comparison as a signal, not proof. One Infection Prevention lead owns the tally and huddle update, with a 45-minute weekly admin budget outside existing audit rounds.

At each ward’s end date, remove the prize but keep the simple logging screen and feedback. Four weeks later, use **verified logging coverage** to decide: **keep** the workflow at 80% or higher, **change** it at 60–79%, or **drop** the extra kiosk logging below 60%, subject to the clinical safety gate. If the sample is thin, wait for the next routine audit rather than guessing or extending prizes. Routine hand hygiene and monthly audits continue regardless.
