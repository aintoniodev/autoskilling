## One working day for a useful code review

Give engineers a reliable answer on a pull request within one working day. Before launch, spend 30 minutes of paid time with a few frequent authors and reviewers to find out where reviews stall. Start with a rotating primary reviewer and backup, a protected daily review block, and an oldest-first queue in the team’s existing workflow. Tell the team what they reported and what changed. Run the fixes and feedback for two weeks before adding the reward.

## What earns the reward

**Launch rule:** “Each pull request counts once when a reviewer gives it a useful answer within one working day. If at least 80% get that answer during your group’s four-week window, everyone in the group earns a paid half-day off.”

A useful answer is a submitted approval that identifies what the reviewer checked, or a submitted review that identifies a concrete issue and gives the author a next step. Both count equally. Emoji, acknowledgements, and standalone comments earn no credit. The lead announces the half-day at launch, confirms it in person at the end, and schedules it with normal coverage. People on leave earn it with their group.

## How it’s measured

Use the repository’s existing pull request and submitted-review records. The clock starts when a pull request is ready for review and a human review is requested. One working day means eight hours on a published team calendar that excludes weekends and team holidays. Measure by the week the request became eligible and wait until every request in that cohort has had its full response window.

- A second reviewer, a reassignment, or a reopen creates no extra credit and never restarts the clock.
- A duplicate or genuinely abandoned request withdrawn before its deadline is excluded. A request withdrawn after it misses the deadline stays in the denominator. The programme owner checks exclusions weekly for patterns.
- A review submitted through the team’s existing workflow counts if it meets the same rule. A private answer can unblock the author, and the reviewer can record its substance in the pull request so it counts.

Each week, a rotating senior engineer samples 10 credited reviews, or all of them if there are fewer than 10. If more than 10% fail the useful-answer rule, the engineering lead pauses reward accrual. The programme owner corrects the counts, including any errors engineers flag privately, and accrual resumes once a fresh sample passes. Existing merge and approval requirements stay in force.

## What people see

After a qualifying review, the reviewer gets a private confirmation with the pull request and response time. Reviewers see their own assigned volume and how many requests they answered on time. Their personal target is 80% of the requests they’re assigned while on coverage, so a quiet week carries no penalty and the next request is a fresh chance. The team sees a weekly aggregate in its usual channel. Managers see team totals only, with no individual drill-down or use in performance reviews.

## Timing and ending

Use two groups based on existing review ownership. Randomly choose which group starts first and start the second two weeks later. Each gets four reward weeks. Both groups get the workflow fixes and private feedback from day one. Compare their on-time rates during the stagger as a rough read on what the reward adds. Shared reviewers and shifts in workload can blur that comparison.

Recalculate the baseline from recent repository data using the same working-day rule. The current 2.6-day median gives context, and the **percentage of eligible pull requests with a useful answer within one working day** drives the decision. End prizes on schedule and keep the protected review block, backup coverage, and oldest-first queue as defaults. Measure again over the next four weeks. At **80% or higher**, keep the workflow as is. At **65–79%**, adjust coverage or routing and recheck. Below **65%**, drop the reward approach and diagnose where requests still wait.

The main failure modes are rubber-stamp approvals, reviewers picking easy requests, and review work spilling into evenings. The quality sample catches shallow reviews, oldest-first routing gives harder requests a place in the queue, and the working-hours clock plus backup coverage makes evenings and absences irrelevant to the deadline.
