Give the 25 engineers protected review time and a clear owner for every review-ready PR. Ask five engineers to walk through a delayed review and choose a reward they’d actually use. Start with two 20-minute review blocks per engineer each week, backed by a rotating reviewer who checks the queue every working day and covers absences. Put the oldest unanswered PR first in the team’s existing review queue. Tell the team which delays they reported and what changed.

**Reward.** Offer one hour of paid time off, earned once during each engineer’s two-week reward window. Name the reward at launch and have the team lead confirm it privately when earned. The rule is simple: “Answer your review-ready PRs within one working day with an approval, a specific change request, or a documented blocker and next step.” Set a private goal based on expected assignments and review complexity. For example, the goal might be four timely reviews for someone expected to receive five routine PRs, or one for someone assigned a complex review. Confirm goals before the window opens and adjust for leave or lower-than-expected assignment volume. The next eligible review can count after a miss. Everyone earns independently.

**Measurement.** Use existing PR and review records. The clock starts when a PR is marked ready for review and runs for eight working hours on the team calendar. The queue owner assigns a reviewer and backup promptly. Reassignment never resets the clock. Count the first qualifying response once per PR, credited to the reviewer who does the work. A safety hold with a concrete reason and next step qualifies. A reaction, a generic acknowledgment, or a review of a draft earns no credit. Include overdue and unassigned PRs in the team measure so the queue can’t improve by leaving work unclaimed. Exclude PRs withdrawn before their deadline and check for unusual growth in withdrawals.

**What engineers see.** Give each reviewer prompt, private confirmation that shows the PR, whether it counted, and progress toward the goal. Show only the team’s weekly on-time percentage and eligible PR count in the channel engineers already use. Managers see those same totals. Individual counts stay out of performance reviews. Participation in the reward is optional, and the review workflow applies to everyone.

**Run and end.** Calculate the previous four weeks’ on-time rate from PR records. The current 2.6-day median provides context. Give everyone the queue fixes and feedback for two weeks. Then start the two-week reward with one review rotation in weeks 3–4 and the other in weeks 5–6. Assign the order by lottery and keep the queues and backup coverage in place throughout. Before the rewards end, make reviewer routing and the oldest-first view the defaults. Four weeks after the last reward, use one decision measure: the share of eligible PRs answered within one working day over those four weeks. Keep the workflow at 85% or above. At 70–84%, fix the largest remaining delay and recheck. Below 70%, drop the incentive model and revisit review capacity.

**Owner’s note.** The review rotation lead can run this in under an hour a week. Sample five credited reviews against the team’s existing review standard. If two are rubber stamps or vague holds, pause new payouts, correct the counts, and resume after the next sample passes. Engineers can send the lead a PR link to correct a missed or mistaken count. Watch for easy PRs being picked first, complex PRs aging, and review work crowding out delivery. Use queue order and backup coverage to correct those problems. Compare the rotations in weeks 3–4 using the same on-time rate and eligible counts, alongside each rotation’s two-week rate with fixes and feedback alone. With 25 engineers and shared coverage, treat the comparison as directional.
