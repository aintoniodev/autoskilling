# Hand-hygiene kiosk pilot

**Make the kiosk quick to use, give nurses paid time to learn it, and use the existing monthly audits to decide whether to keep it.** Run an eight-week pilot on all three wards. The kiosk confirms each saved entry immediately. At the end of the pilot, the nursing director gives specific thanks at each ward huddle for a workflow problem staff identified and helped fix.

### What earns recognition

Recognize concrete improvements, such as getting a kiosk relocated so nurses can log without leaving their normal route. Give every nurse protected practice time during their shift. Keep prizes, points and individual targets out of this pilot. The kiosk can confirm that an entry was saved, while the monthly observer audits measure whether hand hygiene actually happened. A prize based on kiosk entries would reward the easiest thing to count.

### What nurses see

Before launch, the ward educators walk the workflow with nurses from day and night shifts. Fix access and placement problems first, then show the changes at shift huddles. Make the logging screen the kiosk default, keep the entry short, and confirm a successful save clearly so a slow response does not prompt a second submission. Patient care takes priority whenever logging would interrupt it.

Nurses see confirmation of their own entry. At the usual ward huddle, everyone sees their ward’s latest audited compliance, the number of opportunities observed, and one kiosk issue fixed or still open. Managers see ward totals only. Keep individual activity out of performance reviews and avoid collecting patient identifiers or creating personal activity reports.

### How it ends

Use the two most recent monthly audits as each ward’s baseline. Launch the fixes and feedback on all three wards together, review the routine audits around weeks four and eight, and check again four weeks after the pilot ends. Keep the kiosk’s short entry flow and save confirmation in place after week eight. The follow-up checks whether the result holds once launch attention has passed.

The decision measure is **observed hand-hygiene compliance**, calculated as compliant opportunities divided by opportunities observed. Keep the kiosk on wards where compliance is at or above baseline at the follow-up and nurses can use it without interrupting care. If compliance falls, fix the workflow and reassess. If the workflow continues to interrupt care, remove that logging step. Compare each ward with its own baseline using the same audit method. If the sample is too small or the shift mix has changed materially, wait for the next routine audit before calling the result. This comparison shows what happened during the rollout. It cannot establish that the kiosk caused the change.

### Owner’s note

The infection prevention lead owns the pilot and uses up to 45 minutes a week to review ward feedback, route fixes, and coordinate the huddle update. Nurses can flag an incorrect confirmation or a failed save through their ward educator, who routes it to the kiosk support team. Rising entry totals alongside flat or falling audited compliance should trigger a check for duplicate saves, logging friction, or pressure to record more. A drop of **five percentage points or more** from a ward’s baseline triggers an immediate review with infection prevention and a pause in pilot promotion on that ward. Any report that logging delayed care triggers a workflow review that shift.
