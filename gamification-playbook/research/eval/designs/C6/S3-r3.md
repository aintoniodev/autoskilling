## Three-ward hand-hygiene pilot

**Make logging quick and safe, then recognise hand hygiene verified by the hospital’s existing monthly audit.** Give nurses paid time to learn the kiosk and have ward leaders deliver specific thanks at handover when audits show good practice. Keep the pilot free of points and prizes. The kiosk records what nurses report, and the monthly audit verifies a sample of care. Together, those records support ward-level recognition but cannot verify individual rewards within a week or two.

### What nurses experience

Before launch, give a few nurses on each ward, including night-shift nurses, 15 minutes of protected time to try the kiosk during a typical shift. Ask where logging would interrupt care and what would make the kiosk easier to use. Fix the biggest barrier before launch and report the change at handover. Make the log screen the kiosk’s default, with a clear way to correct an accidental duplicate. If a moment is unsafe or impractical to log, nurses continue care and resume logging at the next suitable moment. There is no catch-up quota.

After an entry, the nurse sees **“Entry saved”** and can correct their own submission. At the existing ward huddle, staff see the monthly audit result with its sample size, a specific example of good practice from the audit when available, and one kiosk issue the ward has fixed. Managers see the same ward-level results. Pilot data stays out of performance reviews. There are no named rankings, individual targets, or comparisons between wards.

### What counts and how the pilot ends

The monthly observer audit is the measure of real work. Count each observed hand-hygiene opportunity once, using the hospital’s existing audit definitions. The primary measure is **missed opportunities divided by observed opportunities**. Use kiosk records to spot workflow issues such as duplicate submissions and downtime. Entry volume earns nothing and stays out of the compliance calculation.

Run the pilot for **12 weeks** across all three wards, using the two preceding monthly audits as baseline. Give each ward the kiosk fixes and feedback from launch. End the pilot-specific recognition at week 12, leave the log screen as the default, and check the next monthly audit about four weeks later.

Use a **20% relative reduction in the missed-opportunity rate** as the proposed continuation threshold. For example, a baseline of 10 missed opportunities per 100 observed would need to fall to 8 or fewer. Keep the kiosk workflow if the reduction is present at the end of the pilot and at follow-up, with no ward worsening. If results are mixed, fix the workflow issue and reassess. If comparable audits show no sustained improvement, drop the rollout. This comparison shows whether improvement persisted during the pilot. It cannot isolate the effect of recognition.

### Owner’s note

The infection-prevention lead owns the pilot and uses the existing audit process. Budget up to an hour a week for reviewing kiosk issues, correcting disputed counts with the ward’s audit lead, and preparing the huddle update. Compare audit samples with similar shift and opportunity mixes. Mark thin or materially different samples as inconclusive.

Watch for bulk entries, uneven kiosk access, and staff interrupting care to log. Rising entry counts alongside flat or worsening audit results trigger a workflow review. Pause the kiosk on any ward if staff report that logging delays or interrupts care. If a comparable monthly audit shows that a ward’s missed-opportunity rate has risen by **5 percentage points** from baseline, pause celebratory messaging there and investigate with ward staff. There are no payouts to administer.
