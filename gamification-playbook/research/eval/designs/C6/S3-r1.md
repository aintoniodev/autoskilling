## Three-ward hand-hygiene kiosk pilot

Make the kiosk quick to use after care, give nurses paid time to try it, and show them which problems their feedback helped fix. Offer prompt confirmation for each entry and specific thanks for verified workflow improvements. Keep prizes off the pilot. Monthly observers can estimate ward-level hand-hygiene compliance, but they cannot verify which individual kiosk entries reflect real events. A prize tied to entries would make extra logging the easiest way to earn it.

### What nurses see

- **Before launch.** The infection prevention lead walks through a typical shift with nurses from days, nights, and weekends on each ward. Give each nurse 15 minutes of protected paid time to try the kiosk. Resolve access, location, and duplicate paperwork before launch. Post fixes at the existing ward huddle so staff can see what changed.
- **During a shift.** Open the kiosk directly to the ward’s entry screen, with the ward prefilled and no patient identifiers. When care allows, record each opportunity once and use the actual outcome, including a missed action if that is what happened. Show “Entry saved” immediately and offer a way to correct an error. Patient care comes first. After a busy shift, outage, or absence, resume with the next opportunity.
- **At the huddle.** Share the ward’s monthly observed compliance as a percentage and a count, alongside one issue raised and its resolution. Thank nurses for specific, confirmed improvements, such as spotting an access problem that was fixed for night shift. Ask before naming anyone. Nurses can review their own entries privately. Managers receive ward totals only, and kiosk data stays out of performance reviews.

### Timing and ending

Use each ward’s latest comparable monthly audit as its baseline. Run the pilot for eight weeks across all three wards, with the same access fixes and feedback from day one. By week six, make the kiosk the default route for any logging the hospital decides to retain and remove any equivalent duplicate form. At week eight, end launch reminders and keep the ordinary monthly audit feedback. Check compliance again at the first routine audit at least four weeks later.

### Owner’s note

The infection prevention lead owns the pilot, with a 30-minute weekly review of kiosk issues and ward totals. The lead handles correction requests privately and routes access or usability problems to the kiosk owner. Use aggregate kiosk activity to spot outages or uneven uptake. The decision measure is **observer-audited compliance by ward**, calculated as observed hand-hygiene actions divided by observed opportunities. Keep audit sampling comparable across shifts and show the denominator. Observer presence and small samples can distort apparent changes. [WHO hand-hygiene monitoring guidance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK144028/)

Use a five-percentage-point drop from baseline as a review trigger. Pause the kiosk rollout immediately if staff report that logging delayed care. At follow-up, keep the kiosk where compliance is at or above baseline and nurses confirm the workflow fits their shifts. Fix and retest where compliance slips, the workflow adds burden, or the audit sample is too thin to judge. Drop routine kiosk logging on a ward if a decline of five points or more persists through a comparable repeat audit after fixes. Rising kiosk counts alone never override an audit decline.
