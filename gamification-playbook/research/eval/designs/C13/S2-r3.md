## One-working-day code reviews

Give every ready pull request a useful first review within eight working hours. Run the trial through the existing review queue and use paid time as the team reward.

### Make reviews easier first

In week 1, ask six engineers across time zones and seniority levels what makes reviews slow or risky. Give them 30 minutes during work to map the friction. Set up a rotating primary reviewer and backup, protect a daily review block, and put the oldest ready PR first in the existing queue. Make the queue the default starting point for reviewers before rewards begin. Tell the team which changes came from their feedback. Use the first two weeks to check that coverage and routing work across both review groups.

### What earns credit

Tell engineers the rule at launch: **A ready PR counts once when a human reviewer submits a substantive approval or requests changes within eight working hours.**

Use existing PR and review events. The clock starts when a PR first becomes ready for human review, even if assignment is delayed. Count hours against the agreed team working calendar, excluding weekends and holidays. The reviewer rotation covers individual absences. An approval should say what was checked, and a request for changes should identify a concrete issue. The system counts formal review events and the quality sample checks substance.

Include all eligible PRs whose deadline has passed in the denominator, including unanswered PRs and PRs merged without review. Test the query before launch with duplicate reviews, a dismissed approval, a Friday handoff, a draft reopened as ready, and a PR merged through the old workflow. Duplicate reviews and reassignment leave the original clock and count intact. A dismissed review loses credit unless another qualifying review was submitted by the original deadline. Exclude genuinely withdrawn work and bot-only PRs under the same rules used for the baseline.

### What people see and earn

After a qualifying review, send a private confirmation through the existing review notification channel. Give each engineer a private target based on their review volume and coverage days. For someone who typically handles five reviews, the target is four on time. For someone who handles one or two, use their next few assigned reviews across weeks. After time away, pick up with the next review.

Post the team’s weekly on-time rate, numerator, and denominator where engineers already check the queue. Managers see the team totals only. Individual counts stay private and out of performance reviews.

Announce the reward at launch: **90 minutes of paid time off for everyone in a review group that reaches 80% on time across its four reward weeks**, subject to the quality gate. Confirm with engineers in week 1 that they value the time and can realistically take it. The engineering lead thanks the group in person for a specific improvement and schedules coverage so everyone can use the time.

### Timing and decision

Give both groups the workflow fixes and private feedback in weeks 1–2. Start the four-week reward window for one group in week 3 and the other in week 5. Use existing review ownership to define the groups, assign the start order by coin flip, and calculate a four-week pre-launch baseline from PR history. During weeks 3–4, compare each group with its own baseline to see whether the reward adds anything while both groups have the same workflow fixes. Shared reviewers and small PR volumes limit what this comparison can establish.

Rewards end after week 8. Keep the review block, routing, and oldest-first queue in place. In weeks 11–12, use the **share of eligible PRs with a qualifying review within eight working hours** to decide what to keep. At 80% or higher, keep the workflow as standard. At 70–79%, adjust the biggest remaining bottleneck and recheck. Below 70%, retire the incentive programme and revisit how review work is staffed.

### What could go wrong

- **Rubber-stamping or token change requests.** A rotating peer samples eight reviews per week, including fast approvals, change requests, and complex PRs. If two of eight miss an obvious material issue or give the author no actionable answer, the engineering lead pauses the reward and fixes the underlying review problem before resuming it.
- **Easy work gets picked first or urgent work jumps the queue.** Keep overdue PRs visible to the team and check the oldest cases for ownership, complexity, and legitimate urgency. The backup picks up work when the primary is overloaded.
- **A wrong count undermines trust.** Engineers can flag a PR privately to the programme owner, who checks the original events and corrects the affected totals. Budget 35 minutes for sampling and 20 minutes for reporting and corrections each week.
