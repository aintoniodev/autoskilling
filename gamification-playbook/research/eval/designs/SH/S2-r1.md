# One working day for a useful code review

Run a team pilot with a shared reward. Give reviewers a protected review slot each working day and name a backup for absences. Before launch, spend 20 minutes with engineers who regularly author and review PRs to find where reviews stall. Fix the biggest blocker first, such as unclear ownership or requests scattered across channels. Tell the team what they raised and what changed.

## What earns the reward

**A PR counts once when a human reviewer submits a substantive approval or a request for changes within eight team working hours of the PR becoming ready for review.** A request for changes should identify what the author needs to address. An approval is valid without a written comment when the reviewer has checked the change and found no issues.

Each participating group earns one protected hour of paid learning time per engineer if at least **85% of its eligible PRs** receive a qualifying review on time over its four reward weeks. Everyone in the group earns it together. A busy week, a missed review or an absence carries no individual penalty.

## How it’s measured

Use the code host’s existing ready-for-review and submitted-review timestamps. Publish one team working calendar in advance. A PR marked ready at 4 p.m. on a Tuesday is due at 4 p.m. on Wednesday if both are normal working days. Drafts and bot-authored PRs are outside the measure. Use the original ready timestamp so retargeting, rebasing or reopening a PR cannot restart the clock. Include overdue PRs with no response in the denominator. Exclude genuinely withdrawn changes and spot-check those exclusions.

Before launch, replay the rule on a duplicate review, a withdrawn PR, a Friday request, an unanswered PR and a review submitted through the team’s existing workflow. The engineering lead owns the rule and reward decision. The pilot owner checks disputed counts against the PR history, corrects the tally and tells the person who raised the issue.

## What people see

Use the team’s existing review queue and notifications. When a qualifying review lands, privately confirm to the reviewer that their review counted. Engineers can see their own pending requests and due times. Post the group’s weekly percentage, numerator and denominator in the engineering channel where the team already coordinates. Managers receive aggregate results only. Keep pilot data out of performance reviews and omit named rankings.

## How it runs and ends

- **Weeks 1–2:** Give everyone the workflow fixes, review time and feedback. Recalculate the prior four weeks using the same rule to establish a baseline. The current 2.6-day median describes the problem, while the pilot decision uses the share answered within one working day.
- **Weeks 3–8:** Use two existing ownership groups with comparable PR volume and limited overlap. Pick the start order by coin flip. The first group earns toward the reward in weeks 3–6 and the second in weeks 5–8. The two-week gap gives a directional read on what the reward adds while both groups have the same workflow support.
- **Weeks 9–12:** End the reward. Keep the ready-for-review queue ordered by oldest request, with its link pinned and backup routing in place. Use the one-working-day rate across these four weeks to decide. **Keep** the workflow if it reaches 85% and passes the quality check. **Change** the workflow if the rate improves over baseline but misses 85%, or if quality slips. **Drop** the campaign if the rate returns to baseline or worse. Any new reward requires a fresh decision.

## What could go wrong

Rubber-stamping is the biggest risk. Each week, a rotating senior engineer samples five counted reviews across active groups. If two or more are clearly perfunctory or miss an obvious blocking issue, the engineering lead pauses reward accrual, shares the pattern without names and resumes after the next sample passes. Watch for authors delaying the ready marker, splitting PRs to lift the count, or relying on the same fast reviewers. Review suspicious shifts in draft time or PR volume using aggregate data, and use backup coverage when the queue piles up. Keep tallying, sampling and posting within one hour a week.
