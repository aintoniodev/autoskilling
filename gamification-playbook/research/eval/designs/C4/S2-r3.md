# One working day to a useful code review

**Fix the queue before offering a reward.** In week 1, use a 30-minute protected session with six engineers who regularly write and review PRs. Walk through recent slow reviews and ask what made them hard to pick up. Give each work group a named reviewer and backup on rotation, protect a daily review block by reducing their sprint load, and route ready PRs to an oldest-first queue in the tool they already use. Show what changed: “You told us requests went to people on leave; the backup now gets them.” If the queue still lacks coverage, fix that before starting the reward window.

**Reward a review that moves the PR forward.** The rule engineers see: “A PR counts once when a reviewer submits a useful review within one working day: an accountable approval, or feedback that tells the author what needs to change.” Acknowledgements, emoji, automated reviews and reassignments do not count. The target is a first review, not a merge or final approval. If a work group reaches **80% on time** across its three-week reward window and passes the quality check, everyone in that group gets one hour of protected, paid learning time, with sprint work reduced to make room. Confirm with engineers up front that they’d actually value it. No individual prizes or streaks.

**Use the existing PR history.** Start the clock at the first ready-for-review event, or at opening for a non-draft PR. One working day is eight hours on the team’s published coverage calendar; nights, weekends and team holidays don’t count. Report the share of eligible PRs with a qualifying review by the deadline, grouped by when the deadline falls. Unanswered and late PRs are misses, including those nobody picked up. Drafts and bot-authored PRs are out; closing a ready PR without review does not erase it. Reconstruct the previous four weeks with this rule: the reported 2.6-day median is context, not the baseline for the new percentage.

**Show progress without scorekeeping.** The reviewer gets a private confirmation in the existing review notification flow: “PR #123 reviewed in 5 working hours; provisionally counted.” Their personal goal is to clear the reviews due in their own queue during their protected block or ask the backup for help. It scales with actual assignments; there’s no fixed quota or penalty for time off. Post the work group’s weekly rate and denominator in its usual engineering channel. Managers see aggregates only, with no person-level drill-down, and pilot data stays out of performance reviews.

**Catch gaming before it earns anything.** Before launch, replay a duplicate review, a dismissed approval before the deadline, a plain “looking now” comment, and a valid review done outside the new queue. Expect one count, no count unless replaced on time, no count, and one count, respectively. Each week a rotating peer checks five counted reviews across both groups, including a complex PR when available, for unsupported approvals or feedback the author can’t act on. Use the same rubric on a pre-pilot sample. **Two failures out of five pause rewards.** The engineering lead decides when to resume; the pilot steward corrects wrong counts through a private channel. Keep the review rules and merge protections unchanged. If reviewers cherry-pick small PRs or the oldest work keeps ageing, use the protected block and backup to cover it rather than adding a bonus. Budget 45 minutes a week for sampling, corrections and the aggregate post.

**Stagger the reward, then stop it.** Both existing work groups get the fixes and feedback in weeks 1–2. By coin toss, one gets the reward window in weeks 3–5 and the other in weeks 6–8. Compare each group’s on-time rate with its own fixes-only rate, and use the first window’s not-yet-rewarded group as a comparison. Report volumes and treat the result as directional, especially if groups share reviewers.

All prizes end after week 8. By then the oldest-first queue, backup routing and protected review block are the defaults. Four weeks later, use the **same on-time rate** to decide: **keep** the workflow at 80% or better; **change** routing or capacity if it’s below 80% but above the historical baseline; **drop** the pilot-specific process if it’s no better than baseline. Don’t extend prizes to rescue a weak result.
