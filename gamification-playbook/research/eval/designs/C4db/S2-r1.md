# One-working-day code reviews

**Make time for reviews first.** Ask five engineers, including frequent reviewers and people who often wait for reviews, where requests stall. Give them 30 minutes of paid time to test the new workflow. Start with a rotating reviewer and backup for each existing ownership group, a protected review block each working day, and a link to the oldest ready requests in the team’s usual channel. Let engineers swap coverage when they’re out or on incident duty. Tell the team which reported bottlenecks you fixed before the pilot.

**Reward the first substantive review.** The rule engineers see is “A ready PR earns one credit when a teammate submits a substantive review within eight team working hours.” An approval qualifies if the reviewer checked the change. A request for changes qualifies if it names a concrete issue and what the author should address. Authors can keep their usual handoff, including asking in chat, and the reviewer records the decision in the code host. Reviewers can take more time when they need it. A late, careful review is preferable to a rushed approval.

**Use existing PR events.** Start the clock when a PR is both ready and has a review requested. Count eight hours on the team’s published working calendar, skipping nights, weekends, and holidays. Use the first qualifying review timestamp and count each PR once. Reassignments, repeat requests, and reopening do not reset the clock. A dismissed or withdrawn review loses its credit. Exclude PRs the author withdraws before the deadline without a review, and show the aggregate exclusion count alongside the rate. Before launch, test the rule against a duplicate request, a dismissed approval, a Friday request, and a review arranged in chat. Measure the baseline one-day response rate from existing PR events. The current 2.6-day median provides context for the pilot.

**Keep feedback practical and the reward modest.** Engineers see their own pending reviews with due times and get private confirmation when a review earns credit. On coverage days, the personal goal is to answer assigned requests by their deadlines, with the backup taking overflow. A missed review carries no penalty into the next one. Post the team’s weekly on-time rate and eligible PR count in its usual channel. Managers get the same aggregate view. Keep individual counts out of performance reviews. When a group reaches 80% across any two consecutive reward weeks, everyone in that group earns one paid hour to use for learning or a personal engineering project. Award it once per group.

**Run an eight-week pilot.** In weeks 1–2, everyone gets the coverage, queue, and feedback. Randomly choose which of two existing ownership groups starts earning the reward in week 3. The other starts in week 5. Each group gets four reward weeks. Use weeks 3–4 to see whether the early group improves more than the later group, treating the comparison as directional given the team’s size. After week 8, end the reward and keep the oldest ready requests at the top of the default review queue. In weeks 9–12, use the **percentage of eligible PRs with a substantive review within one working day** to decide what stays. At 80% or higher, keep the workflow and retire the reward. Below 80% but above baseline, fix the largest remaining bottleneck. At or below baseline, drop the incentive and revisit the coverage design.

**Watch for rushed approvals, deadline resets, and overloaded reviewers.** Each week, the pilot owner samples five credited PRs, or all of them if there are fewer, for evidence of a substantive review. If two or more fail, the owner pauses reward accrual, checks the cause with reviewers, and resumes once it’s fixed. Engineers flag miscounts privately to the owner, who corrects them from the PR history. If requests keep piling up with a specialist, rebalance coverage or train a backup. Budget 45 minutes a week for sampling, corrections, and the team update.
