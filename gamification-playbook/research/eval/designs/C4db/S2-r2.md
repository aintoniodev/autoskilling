## One working day for a useful code review

Run a six-week pilot for the 25-engineer product team. The goal is for at least 85% of review requests to receive a useful first response within one working day. The current median is 2.6 days. Before launch, use the last four weeks of pull requests to establish the baseline for the one-day target.

### Make reviews easier to pick up

Ask five engineers who regularly author and review pull requests where reviews get stuck. Use their feedback to fix routing and agree what authors should provide up front. Give the team a short paid walkthrough of the new workflow. Put the oldest unanswered requests first in the existing review queue, assign a primary reviewer and backup through the existing ownership rules, and protect a daily review block. Show the team what changed in response to their feedback.

### Count one useful response per pull request

The rule engineers see is “A pull request counts once when a reviewer submits an approval or actionable changes requested within eight team working hours of its first ready-for-review request.”

Use the Git platform’s existing review-request and submitted-review timestamps, with an agreed team calendar that excludes evenings, weekends, and team holidays. Drafts enter the cohort when they are ready and review is requested. The weekly denominator is all eligible requests whose deadlines fell that week, including requests still unanswered at the deadline. A late review helps the author and stays recorded as late.

Before launch, replay cases from the repository. A second approval earns nothing extra. A dismissed review is removed from the tally, and a replacement counts only if it met the original deadline. Reassigning a reviewer, reopening a pull request, or toggling draft status leaves the clock unchanged. Bot reviews, self-reviews, chat replies, and merges without a qualifying review earn no credit. Walk through a chat-only review with the team so reviewers know to submit their decision on the pull request where the author can act on it.

### Give the team feedback and a modest reward

Use the existing review notification as immediate confirmation. If the team’s chat integration supports private replies, tell the reviewer “First review submitted in 5 working hours.” Reviewers can see their own eligible requests and timely responses privately. The personal target is four in five across the pilot, with no minimum review count. Someone who gets two requests has two chances to help, and a missed week carries no penalty.

Post the weekly team percentage and raw counts in the engineering channel. Managers get the same aggregate, with no individual drill-down. Keep programme data out of performance reviews. Skip points, streaks, badges, and rankings.

Split the pilot across two existing review rotations with distinct queues. Both get the workflow fixes and private feedback from day one. The first rotation’s reward window is weeks 2–4, and the second’s is weeks 4–6. If a rotation answers at least 80% of eligible requests on time across its three weeks and clears the quality check, everyone in it gets one paid hour for learning or personal engineering work. Compare the rotations’ changes from baseline during weeks 2–3 to see whether the reward coincided with an extra lift. Cross-rotation reviewing can blur that comparison.

### Protect quality and end on schedule

Each week the rotation lead samples five counted reviews across the team, or all of them if there are fewer. Check whether approvals are defensible and requests for changes give the author a concrete next step. If two or more fail, the engineering lead pauses reward qualification while the team fixes the issue. Engineers can send a pull request link privately to the rotation lead for a count correction. Budget 45 minutes a week for sampling, corrections, and posting totals.

Watch for rushed approvals, pressure to keep pull requests in draft, and review work piling up on a few specialists. Use the sample to catch rubber-stamping, ask authors about delayed requests in the existing retrospective, and have backups take over when specialists are overloaded.

End rewards after week six and keep the queue order, backup routing, and protected review time in place. Four weeks later, use the share of eligible requests answered on time as the decision measure. At 85% or above, keep the workflow as the default. At 70–84%, fix the biggest remaining delay and recheck. Below 70%, retire the programme and revisit review capacity and routing.
