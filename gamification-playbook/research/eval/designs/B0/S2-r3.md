# One working day to a useful code review

**Goal:** Give at least 90% of review-ready pull requests a useful first response within one working day. That is a commitment to help the author move forward, not a deadline to approve or merge. The current median is 2.6 days; recalculate it using the pilot’s clock before comparing results.

## What gets rewarded

Reward the team for reliable, useful reviews, not individuals for speed or volume. During the incentive phase, if at least 90% of PRs are answered on time over a two-week block and at least 9 of 10 sampled responses are useful, cancel one 30-minute team status meeting and give everyone the time back as a protected, agenda-free block. Post essential updates asynchronously. No points, rankings, streaks, prizes for approvals, or use in performance reviews.

Give the team capacity to succeed whether or not it earns the reward. Each week, rotate a queue owner and backup across the 25 engineers, skipping people on leave or on call. The owner checks the queue twice daily, assigns a qualified reviewer, and uses the backup if no one can take it. Reserve two 30-minute review windows per working day for the duty pair and reduce their planned feature work accordingly. If demand exceeds capacity, the engineering manager moves work or adds coverage rather than expecting overtime.

## How it is measured

- **Start:** When a human-authored PR first becomes ready for review, even if no reviewer has been assigned. Count eight hours on an agreed team coverage calendar, excluding nights, weekends, and published team holidays. Reassignment and moves back to draft do not reset the clock.
- **Stop:** At the first submitted human review that gives the author something useful: an informed approval, a specific change request or question, or, for a complex PR, a concrete finding plus a named next step and follow-up time. “Looking,” bot comments, self-reviews, and bare acknowledgements do not count.
- **Score:** For PRs whose deadline falls in the reporting period, show the count and percentage answered on time. Unanswered PRs count as misses once due. Exclude PRs genuinely withdrawn before the deadline, but publish the exclusion count and sample the reasons. Count each PR once.
- **Check quality and downstream delay:** At each two-week checkpoint, a rotating engineer and the engineering manager sample ten first responses, including complex PRs and approvals, and check whether the feedback is grounded in the diff and gives a clear disposition or next step. If fewer than ten are available, audit all and require at least 90% to pass. Also track the 90th-percentile time to a complete review decision and whether promised follow-ups happen. A faster first reply is not a win if the real review takes longer.

## What people see

In the existing PR tool, show the live queue with reviewer, age, and due time. Send one private nudge to the queue owner when two working hours remain. In the team channel, post one weekly aggregate: on-time count and percentage, overdue count, median and 90th-percentile first-response times, and any quality or downstream issues. Names appear only where needed to route active work, never in a scorecard.

## Duration and exit

Run for **eight weeks**. In weeks 1–2, replay the previous four weeks of PR events, agree on the clock and quality rubric, and check that the queue and reporting match real PRs. In weeks 3–6, run the rotation and two reward blocks. In weeks 7–8, remove the reward but keep staffing and measurement unchanged.

Keep the rotation and light reporting only if the reward-free phase still reaches 90% on time, passes the quality check, and does not worsen the 90th-percentile time to a complete review decision versus baseline. Otherwise, end the incentive anyway, review the misses, and fix the biggest bottleneck before trying again. Do not extend the contest or raise the prize.

## What could go wrong

- **Rubber-stamping or token replies:** Audit the diff and follow-up, not the presence of a comment. Withhold the reward if the quality check fails.
- **Easy PRs jump the queue; hard ones stall:** Triage oldest first by default, with explicit exceptions for urgent work. Route complex PRs to a qualified reviewer early and check promised follow-ups.
- **The same experts absorb the load:** Review assignments and review time privately. Move feature commitments and pair a backup with the expert rather than sending every hard PR to the same person.
- **The reward becomes peer pressure:** Discuss misses as coverage, routing, or workload problems. If people report pressure to approve, work after hours, or hide PRs, stop the reward early and fix the workflow.
