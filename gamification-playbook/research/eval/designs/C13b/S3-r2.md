## Three-ward kiosk pilot

**Make logging fit the shift.** Give a day- and night-shift nurse from each ward 20 minutes of paid time to try the kiosk during a normal shift. Fix placement, sign-in delays and duplicate paperwork before launching the reward. Put the logging screen first, prefill the ward and shift, and leave the hygiene moment for the nurse to select. Keep a downtime route. Patient care takes priority whenever logging would interrupt it. Post the fixes at the ward huddle so staff can see what changed.

**Reward a usable ward report.** At launch, announce a hospital-funded team meal, with food provided on every shift, when the ward completes its first monthly review with usable kiosk records from at least 80% of eligible shifts. The infection prevention lead checks that the kiosk export covers the correct ward and shifts, that corrections and duplicates are resolved, and that downtime gaps are explained. The report earns credit once, when the lead accepts it for the existing monthly review. Each ward can earn the meal independently. The nursing director brings it to shift huddles and thanks the team for a specific improvement they made.

**Measure adoption and safety.** Calculate adoption as the share of staffed ward shifts with a usable kiosk record, using the existing roster for the denominator and excluding documented kiosk outages. Publish the outage count alongside the rate. A blank shift gets no credit. An accurate zero can count only when the ward can substantiate it through an existing record. Test the rule before launch with a duplicate save, a corrected entry and a paper record from a downtime shift. The kiosk owner fixes count errors before the report closes. The infection prevention lead samples records during the existing monthly review and holds the reward if more than 10% of sampled records have unresolved errors, if observed compliance falls below the hospital’s existing safety floor, or if staff report that logging interfered with care. Set the safety floor before launch if the hospital lacks one.

**Show staff what helps them act.** After saving, show “Saved to this shift” with an undo option. At the existing huddle, show the ward’s progress toward 80%, the latest observed compliance rate with its sample size, and any kiosk fix under way. Managers get ward totals with no individual drill-down. Keep pilot data out of performance reviews and omit patient identifiers from the pilot report. A missed shift stays a missed shift. The next one can still count.

**Stagger the reward and end it on schedule.** Start all three wards with four weeks of kiosk fixes and feedback. Then open an eight-week reward window for each ward, starting four weeks apart in an order drawn before launch. Compare each ward’s adoption with its own baseline and the wards still on feedback alone. Treat the comparison as directional with three wards. Keep the kiosk defaults and feedback when rewards end. Four weeks later, use the same adoption rate to decide the workflow’s future, subject to the safety checks. Keep it at 80% or above, fix and retest it at 60–79%, and drop the pilot reporting requirement below 60%. Use the existing monthly review and an automated weekly total to keep administration under an hour a week.

**Watch for false confidence.** One entry can make a shift look covered, and a high log count can coexist with missed hand hygiene. Use kiosk coverage only to judge adoption. Keep the existing observer audit independent, sample across shifts within the current audit schedule, and report the denominator so a small or unusually timed sample does not drive a reward decision. Observation can itself change behaviour, so treat the audit as a safety check with known limits. [WHO hand hygiene monitoring guidance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK144028/)
