## Code review pilot

Run a four-week pilot for the 25-engineer team. The goal is for every PR to get a substantive first review within one working day. The team earns a modest reward for improving on its current rate.

**Fix the review queue first.** Give five engineers 30 minutes of protected time to walk through recent slow reviews. Include frequent authors, occasional reviewers and engineers across time zones. Fix the biggest bottleneck before starting the pilot and post what changed in the team channel. Set up a rotating primary reviewer and backup, give them a protected review block, and put the oldest ready PRs first in the queue. During the walkthrough, let engineers choose between a team lunch and an hour of protected learning time as the shared reward.

**Reward one useful first review per PR.** “A ready PR earns one credit when a teammate submits a substantive review within eight working hours.” An approval after reading the diff, a request for changes, or specific feedback that lets the author move forward qualifies. A reaction, acknowledgement, or bot comment earns zero credit. A review that starts in chat counts if the reviewer records the decision or actionable feedback on the PR before the deadline.

**Measure from existing PR events.** Start the clock when a PR first becomes ready for review. Count eight hours on the team’s published working calendar, excluding weekends and team holidays. Reassignment, new commits and switching back to draft leave the start time intact. Use the previous four weeks of PR history to establish the share reviewed on time. The current median of 2.6 days gives context. Set the reward threshold 10 percentage points above the measured baseline, capped at 100%. Include every ready PR whose deadline has passed, including unanswered and overdue PRs. Verify genuine duplicates and withdrawals before excluding them. Before launch, walk through a duplicate, a review dismissed as accidental, and a review that happened in chat. An accidental review loses its credit, and the original clock keeps running.

**Show progress where engineers already work.** Use the existing PR notification to confirm qualifying reviews with the PR link and elapsed working time. Give each engineer a private view of their assigned reviews. On rotation, their personal goal is to answer three of their next four assigned PRs on time. At low volume, carry the goal forward across weeks and show progress against the cases they actually received. A missed week simply means picking up the next case. Post the team’s on-time rate, PR count and milestone progress once a week in the engineering channel. Managers see team totals and look at individual records when needed for a specific coaching or correction conversation.

**Watch for rushed reviews and uneven load.** Each week, the engineering lead samples five credited reviews across reviewers and checks whether the response was grounded in the diff and gave the author a usable decision or next step. If two fail, the lead pauses reward eligibility, corrects the credits and addresses the cause before restarting it. Use the existing queue to watch for older or larger PRs being skipped and repeated spillover to the backup. Rebalance assignments or protect more reviewer time when that happens. Pull the weekly numbers from PR history and keep the sample and team update to under an hour.

**End the reward after week four.** Once the final PRs have reached their deadlines, deliver the chosen reward if the team meets the threshold and the quality pause has been cleared. Before the pilot ends, make the reviewer rotation, backup and oldest-first queue part of the normal workflow. For four more weeks, track the same on-time rate and quality sample without prizes. Compare with a similar team if one is available, or with the pre-launch baseline. Keep the workflow if faster reviews hold and quality stays steady. If response times slip or quality falls, use the queue and sample to identify what to change before considering another incentive.
