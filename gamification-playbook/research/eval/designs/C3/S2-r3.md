## One working day for a first review

Run a four-week pilot for the 25-engineer team. Reward a timely first review that gives the author enough information to move the PR forward. Keep the existing review standards and let reviewers request changes when needed.

### Before launch

Give five engineers 30 minutes of protected work time to walk through recent slow reviews. Include frequent authors, frequent reviewers, and someone whose work often gets interrupted. Fix the biggest blocker they identify before launching. For example, if review requests sit with engineers who are away or deep in planned work, set up a rotating backup and reserve a short review window in the workday. Post what engineers reported and what changed. Give everyone 15 minutes of protected time to try the new workflow.

### What counts

**One PR earns credit when a requested reviewer records a substantive approval, change request, or blocking question in the PR within eight team working hours of the first review request on a ready PR.**

A blocking question identifies what the reviewer needs to proceed. An approval explains what was checked or why the change is safe. Use the team’s published work hours and holiday calendar. Requests outside those hours start at the next opening. The backup can answer within the original window.

Use existing PR timestamps and review history. Calculate the weekly rate from all eligible PRs whose response window has elapsed, grouped by request week. Open and late PRs count as misses. Measure the previous four weeks the same way to establish the baseline percentage alongside the current 2.6-day median.

Before launch, test the rule on a few recent PRs. Duplicate requests and reopens keep the original clock and can earn only one credit. An approval withdrawn as premature loses credit unless a qualifying answer was already recorded within the window. A Slack reply counts from the time its actionable substance is recorded in the PR.

### What engineers see and earn

After an eligible response, send the reviewer a private confirmation through the existing PR notification workflow, such as “Answered in 5 working hours. This PR counted toward the team goal.” Engineers see their own eligible requests and results. Offer a personal target sized to the week’s assignments, such as answering one of two on time where both usually run late, or three of four where two usually land on time. A quiet week or an absence carries no penalty. The next request is a fresh chance.

Post the team’s weekly rate and request count in its usual engineering channel. Managers get team totals. The milestone is a pooled rate **10 percentage points above baseline across any two consecutive pilot weeks**, with the quality check passed in both weeks. On reaching it, the team earns one lunch during paid work time, with an equivalent allowance for remote engineers. Confirm with the team before launch that they’d value it.

### Quality, workload, and the ending

The engineering lead spends up to 45 minutes a week pulling the totals, sampling five credited reviews or all of them if fewer, and posting progress. If two sampled reviews lack an actionable answer or skip a required review check, the lead pauses rewards, corrects the tally, and resumes after the next sample passes. Include the age of the oldest unanswered PR in the weekly update so a rising rate can’t hide neglected work. If easy PRs get picked first, put the oldest ready request first in the queue. If the review window keeps spilling into focus time or evenings, adjust backup coverage.

Before the fourth week ends, make the ready-for-review queue and backup assignment the defaults in the existing PR workflow. Then end the reward and its notifications. For four more weeks, track the same response rate, oldest unanswered PR, and sampled quality against the pre-pilot baseline. Keep the workflow if the response rate stays above baseline without weaker reviews or older unanswered PRs. If the gain fades or quality drops, use the team’s feedback to change the workflow and retire the reward.
