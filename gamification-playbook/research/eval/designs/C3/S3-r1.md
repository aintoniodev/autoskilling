## Three-ward hand hygiene pilot

**Set up with nurses.** Spend two weeks testing the kiosk with day, night, and weekend nurses on all three wards. Give them 15 minutes of paid shift time to try it and identify where logging interrupts care. Before launching rewards, fix the biggest barriers they identify, such as a slow sign-in or a kiosk that requires a detour. Report the fixes at handover. Make “Log hand hygiene” the first kiosk action, with the ward prefilled and an undo option. Keep patient identifiers out of the form.

**Reward completed hand hygiene.** Offer each ward one hospital-funded team meal, with equivalent food available to every shift, if it reaches its own audited improvement goal over two monthly audits. Set the goal before launch at three percentage points above the ward’s pooled rate from its last two comparable audits. For a ward already at 97% or higher, use its baseline as the goal. All three wards can earn the reward independently.

Give nurses one sentence for the kiosk: “Log each completed hand-hygiene action once, as soon as care allows.” Test duplicate taps, undone entries, kiosk outages, and an action completed without a kiosk entry before launch. A completed action still counts in the observer audit if the nurse couldn’t log it. After an absence or a missed entry, pick up at the next opportunity.

**Measure adoption and compliance.** Use the existing monthly observer audit for the reward. Divide completed observed actions by observed opportunities and pool the counts across the two pilot audits. Keep the usual observation method and a comparable mix of shifts. Before launch, Infection Prevention sets a minimum observation count based on routine audit coverage. If coverage is insufficient, hold the reward decision until the next routine audit. Observation samples only part of the workday, and people can change their behaviour when observed. [WHO guidance on direct observation](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK144056/)

Use the kiosk’s existing timestamps to report the weekly share of ward shifts with at least one valid entry. Use that measure to spot shifts where logging is hard to use. It can show whether logging is spreading across shifts. Reporting completeness remains unknown. The programme lead checks 10 entries a week across the three wards for confirmed duplicates or undone entries still counted. If two or more fail, Infection Prevention pauses reward decisions, fixes the counting issue, and reviews a fresh sample before resuming. Keep the weekly review under an hour.

**Show progress at handover.** After each save, the kiosk displays “Hand hygiene logged” with the time and an undo button. At the existing staff huddle, show the ward’s logging coverage, latest audited result with its sample size, and its goal. Managers see ward totals. Keep individual entries private except when needed to resolve a specific issue. Use the huddle to ask what made logging hard on shifts with gaps.

**End after two monthly audit cycles.** Award the meal once, then close the incentive. Keep the kiosk shortcut and the workflow fixes in place. For two further monthly audits, compare compliance with each ward’s pre-launch baseline and check whether kiosk use holds across shifts. If logging falls and audited compliance holds, revisit whether the kiosk gives nurses or Infection Prevention enough value to justify the work. If both fall, use the next huddle to identify what changed before deciding what to keep or revise.

**Watch for harm.** Duplicate logs can inflate apparent uptake, and a good audit can hide gaps on less-observed shifts. Review both signals with their limitations visible. If logging delays patient care or staff feel pressured to enter actions they can’t recall, the ward lead pauses the logging campaign and fixes the workflow. Routine hand hygiene and monthly audits continue throughout.
