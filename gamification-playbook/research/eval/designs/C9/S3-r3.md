## Three-ward hand hygiene pilot

**Reward one verified outcome.** Launch with this rule: “If our ward reaches its audit goal for correctly performed, accurately logged hand hygiene, the hospital will provide a catered meal for every shift.” Each ward can earn the meal independently. Serve it during covered breaks so night staff and staff on busy shifts can take part.

**Make logging safe and easy first.** Walk through the workflow with day- and night-shift nurses on all three wards. Ask where logging would interrupt care, what makes the kiosk hard to use, and whether the meal is something they’d value. Give nurses 10 minutes of paid practice. Fix queues, awkward kiosk placement and duplicate entry before launch. Preselect the ward and put the logging action on the first screen. At huddle, show nurses what changed because of their feedback. Follow hospital hand-hygiene protocol and log at the next safe break in care. [CDC hand-hygiene guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/clean-hands/hcp/clinical-safety/)

**Use the existing monthly audit.** On a prespecified subset of routine observations, the observer adds one check. After care, the nurse can show an anonymous saved receipt that matches the observed moment and was saved before the observer asked. Allow verification later in the same shift for deferred entries. Score each eligible observed opportunity once, as verified only when hygiene was performed correctly and the kiosk record matches. Duplicate, unmatched and bulk entries earn no credit. Mark hospital-approved safety exceptions and kiosk outages separately as valid reasons to skip logging. Those opportunities stay in the clinical audit and are excluded from the reward denominator under rules set before launch. Sample across shifts and keep the underlying clinical audit unchanged. [WHO guidance on direct observation](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK144028/)

**Show one ward score.** The kiosk confirms “Saved” and shows the moment recorded so the nurse can correct it. A nurse’s personal goal is to log the next eligible moment at a safe break in care. Missing a shift or a week resets nothing. After each audit, post the ward’s verified percentage, sample size and goal in the usual huddle view. Managers see those same aggregates. Pilot reporting stores no nurse or patient identifiers and has no role in performance reviews. The infection-prevention lead owns corrections through the existing safety channel and runs the pilot in under an hour a week outside routine audits.

**Run a baseline month, then an eight-week reward period per ward.** Give all three wards the workflow fixes at the start of the baseline month. Randomly pick one ward to start the reward two weeks ahead of the other two. Time the first start so a routine audit falls in that two-week window. Use the early comparison as a directional read because three wards give little power to isolate the reward effect. Set each ward’s target before launch at 10 percentage points above its baseline, capped at 90%, or maintenance of baseline if it already exceeds 90%. Pool at least 30 eligible observations per ward before awarding the meal or judging the result.

**Pause for harm and check what lasts.** Any report that logging delayed care, or a drop of five percentage points in observed hand-hygiene compliance versus baseline, pauses the reward while infection prevention investigates. If kiosk volume rises without a rise in verified coverage, check for duplicates, backfilling or logging concentrated around observer visits. End the prize after eight weeks and keep the simplified kiosk workflow in place. At the routine audit about four weeks later, use the same verified percentage to decide. Keep the workflow if the ward still meets its target and safety holds. Fix a specific workflow barrier and retest if the score falls below target. Drop the incentive if it produces no verified lift or causes recurring disruption. An undersized sample carries forward to the next routine audit.
