# One-working-day code reviews

Run a four-week trial for the 25-engineer team. The goal is for authors to get a substantive first response within one working day. Use the existing PR queue and team channel.

## Fix the review queue first

Spend 30 minutes of paid time with five engineers who regularly author or review PRs. Walk through recent slow reviews and fix the biggest sources of delay. Give every ready PR one named reviewer and a backup. Use a rotation to spread the load, and protect a 20-minute review block in each engineer’s working day. The rotation owner routes PRs around leave and flags overload in stand-up so the team can reprioritize. Post what engineers reported and what changed before the trial starts.

## What earns the reward

**A ready PR counts once when a reviewer gives it a substantive first response within eight team working hours.**

An approval after reviewing the change, a specific request for changes, or a question tied to the diff qualifies. An emoji, a routing comment, or a generic acknowledgment earns zero credit. Track time using the team’s agreed work calendar, excluding evenings, weekends and team holidays. Reassigning a reviewer, pushing another commit, or reopening the same change preserves the original clock. A PR closed without a response stays in the denominator unless it was a verified duplicate or withdrawn because the work was canceled. Late reviews still help the author and get the same specific acknowledgment.

If at least 80% of eligible PRs in weeks three and four meet the deadline and quality clears the audit, everyone earns 30 minutes of protected paid learning time the following week. Before launch, check that the team values that reward and have the engineering manager reserve the time. Each engineer’s private goal is to answer at least four out of every five PRs they own on time across the trial. For lighter review loads, show the actual count, such as “2 of 2 on time.” A missed review leaves the next one available to earn credit.

## Measurement and visibility

Use PR events to calculate elapsed working time, the percentage answered on time, and the median and 90th percentile time to first response. Recalculate the previous four weeks using the same rules so the reported 2.6-day median can be compared on the same clock. Include overdue PRs awaiting a response as misses in the on-time rate. Let PRs opened at the end of each reporting window reach their deadline before closing the report.

After a qualifying response, send the reviewer a private confirmation with the PR and elapsed time. Post the team’s aggregate results and outstanding overdue count in its existing channel each week. Managers get team totals and use individual data only for a specific coaching or correction need.

## What could go wrong

Each week, the rotation owner spot-checks five responses provisionally counted as on time, including at least one approval. Remove invalid credits. If two or more of the five are perfunctory or skip an obvious material issue, the engineering manager pauses reward qualification, reviews the examples with the team, and resumes it after a clean five-PR check. Before launch, test the counting rules on a duplicate, a reopened PR, an after-hours request, and a substantive review left as an ordinary PR comment. Count the ordinary comment if it meets the rule.

If specialists become bottlenecks, give them a trained backup and reduce their other assignments. If first responses get faster while time to merge rises, use stand-up to surface stalled follow-up and give each stuck PR an owner. Budget 45 minutes a week for reporting, sampling, and corrections.

## How it ends

By the last week, make reviewer assignment automatic for ready PRs and put the oldest unanswered PRs first in the team’s review queue. Keep the protected review block. End the reward after week four and track the same measures for four more weeks. Keep the queue setup if at least 80% of PRs still get a substantive response within one working day and the quality checks hold. If performance slips, use the overdue PRs to identify which part of routing or workload needs fixing.
