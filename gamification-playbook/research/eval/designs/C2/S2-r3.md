## One-working-day code reviews

Run a four-week pilot for the 25-engineer team. Aim for **at least 80% of review-ready PRs to receive a substantive first review within one working day**. The current median is 2.6 days. Measure the baseline on-time rate as well so the team can see how much the pilot changes it.

### Make reviews easier before launch

Give five engineers across time zones 30 minutes of protected work time to walk through recent slow reviews. Use what they find to fix the biggest delays before starting the pilot. For example, if ownership is unclear, route each ready PR to one available reviewer with a backup. If reviews keep losing to feature work, protect a daily 20-minute review window and adjust sprint commitments to make room. Post the fixes in the team channel and credit the people who reported the problems.

### What earns credit

> A PR earns one credit when someone other than the author gives it a substantive first review within one working day of its first ready-for-review request.

A substantive review is an approval after checking the change, a request for changes with a concrete reason, or a specific question that identifies what the author must clarify before the reviewer can proceed. The clock counts eight hours on a published team working calendar, skips weekends and team holidays, and starts at the next opening for after-hours requests. The backup covers reviewer absences.

Use repository events to record the ready timestamp, first qualifying review, and elapsed working time. Before launch, test the rule against recent PRs and agree on the awkward cases. Duplicate requests and reopened PRs keep the original clock and can earn only one credit. A withdrawn approval keeps its credit if the original review was substantive. A chat reply counts once the reviewer records the substance on the PR, using the repository timestamp. Exclude bots, drafts that never become ready, and PRs withdrawn before the deadline while still awaiting review. Count overdue unanswered PRs as misses.

### What people see and earn

Send the reviewer a private confirmation after each on-time review, with the PR link and elapsed working time. Give each engineer a private weekly goal of answering **four out of every five** eligible reviews routed to them, with the actual count shown so low-volume weeks are easy to interpret. A missed week has no effect on the next one.

Post the team’s weekly on-time rate, eligible PR count, and median response time in the existing engineering channel. Managers get the same team totals and can inspect individual cases for coaching or correction. If the team reaches 80% across the four weeks and passes the quality check, everyone earns one hour of paid time back, built into sprint capacity.

### Quality and failure modes

Each week, the engineering lead samples five credited reviews, including at least one larger PR when available. Check whether the reviewer engaged with the change and raised any obvious issue that should have blocked approval. **If two or more of the five fail, the lead pauses reward accrual**, explains the pattern without naming reviewers, and resumes after a fresh sample passes. The team retains earlier valid credit.

Watch for rubber-stamp approvals, authors delaying the ready signal, and backups absorbing too much work. Use the sample to catch shallow reviews, spot-check long-lived drafts, and rebalance the queue when coverage is uneven. Show the PR count alongside the rate so quiet weeks don’t look like lasting gains. With automated reporting, the lead’s weekly work is the five-review sample, a queue check, and a short update, budgeted at 45 minutes.

### How it ends

Before week four ends, make automatic routing, backup coverage, and oldest-due-first sorting the default in the review queue. End credit notifications and the reward at week four. Track the same team measures for four more weeks against the four weeks before launch. If the on-time rate holds at 80% and quality stays within the guardrail, keep the workflow defaults. If speed or quality slips, use the missed cases to decide which routing or capacity changes to keep, change, or drop.
