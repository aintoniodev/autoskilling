## Three-ward hand hygiene pilot

**Reward the ward for hand hygiene verified in the existing monthly audit.** Each ward can earn one hospital-funded meal, with equivalent options for every shift, by meeting its target across its two reward months. Set the target from that ward’s baseline audit after workflow fixes. Below 95%, aim to close 20% of the gap to 100%, capped at 95%. A ward starting at 80% aims for 84%. Wards starting at 95% or higher aim to stay at or above 95%. Everyone on the ward shares the reward.

**Make the kiosk easy to use first.** Ask nurses from day, night, and weekend shifts to walk through a typical round and identify where logging interrupts care. Give each nurse 15 minutes of protected paid practice. Fix access, placement, and extra taps before starting the reward, and post what changed in response to staff feedback. Open the kiosk directly to a blank logging form and confirm each save immediately. Nurses can correct an entry or resume on their next case after an absence. Patient care comes first, and a kiosk outage never changes the hand hygiene procedure.

**Use the existing audit to score the reward.** Infection Prevention divides observed opportunities where nurses performed hand hygiene correctly by all eligible opportunities observers saw. Count each observed opportunity once, even if several indications apply. Include observed hand hygiene done without a kiosk entry. Duplicate, corrected, or deleted kiosk entries have no effect on the audit score. Before launch, run through those cases plus a missed hand hygiene opportunity with a kiosk entry. That last case stays in the audit denominator and earns no credit. [WHO hand hygiene measurement guidance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK144028/)

**Give staff one ward view where they already look.** On the existing huddle board, show the latest audit result and sample size, the ward target, the next update month, and the meal the team can earn. At the kiosk, nurses see immediate confirmation of their own entry. Managers receive ward totals. Use aggregate kiosk submissions and brief, anonymous staff feedback to spot adoption friction. Keep individual logs out of manager reports and performance reviews. Use no rankings or streaks.

**Run the pilot over six months.** In month 0, give all three wards the same workflow fixes, training, and feedback, then collect their baseline audits. Randomly assign the reward windows to months 1–2, 2–3, and 3–4. Compare changes from baseline in wards with active rewards against wards still waiting during months 1 and 2. Recheck each ward at its first monthly audit after rewards stop. Keep the kiosk as the default logging route and retain the feedback at routine huddles.

**Decide using one outcome.** Use audited hand hygiene compliance to decide what stays. Keep the kiosk workflow if rewarded wards improve more than waiting wards and sustain their targets at follow-up. If compliance improves during rewards and slips afterward, change the workflow and retest. If adequate audits show no improvement, retire the incentive and review whether the kiosk adds enough value to keep. Treat the three-ward comparison as a pilot signal.

**Protect the audit and patient care.** Before launch, Infection Prevention sets a minimum audit sample and shift coverage based on the hospital’s existing audit capacity. Vary observation times within the existing audit schedule, including nights and weekends, because staff may change their behavior when an observer is present. Check a small sample of audit records for duplicate opportunities and unjustified exclusions. The Infection Prevention lead pauses reward decisions if coverage is inadequate or audited compliance falls five percentage points below baseline. The audit lead corrects scoring errors, and the ward’s kiosk owner fixes logging issues. Handle corrections in the existing huddle and audit workflow, with one coordinator spending up to an hour a week.
