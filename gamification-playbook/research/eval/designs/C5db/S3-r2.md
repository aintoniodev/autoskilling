# Hand-hygiene kiosk pilot

**Reward verified hand hygiene at ward level.** Each ward earns one staff-chosen meal, with equivalent options across shifts and for float staff, when it reaches its target in a valid monthly audit during its two-month reward window. Name the meal and the ward’s target at launch. The ward leader delivers the reward in person across shifts and thanks staff for a specific improvement they made. The launch rule is simple: “Your ward earns the meal when the monthly observers confirm that it reached its hand-hygiene target.”

**Make the kiosk easy to use first.** Give day, night, and float nurses protected paid time to try it. Ask what makes recording awkward during a real shift, then fix problems with placement, access, or entry time before rewards begin. Have infection prevention approve kiosk placement and cleaning. Share the fixes at huddles. During urgent care, staff prioritise care and hand hygiene. Follow the ward’s usual process if the kiosk is unavailable.

**Use the existing monthly audit.** Calculate the percentage of eligible observed opportunities where hand hygiene was performed correctly, using the hospital’s existing definitions and sampling protocol. Pool the last two valid audits for each ward’s baseline. For the pilot, set the target five percentage points above baseline, capped at 95%. Wards already at or above 95% aim to stay within two points of baseline and at or above 95%. These are reward thresholds. The hospital’s clinical standards apply throughout.

- A correctly performed moment counts once in the audit, including when the nurse uses the old recording method or misses the kiosk. A duplicate kiosk entry earns no extra credit. A corrected or withdrawn entry leaves the audit score unchanged unless the observer’s record itself was wrong.
- Use aggregate kiosk totals, if already available, to spot changes worth asking staff about. Observers sample some opportunities, so dividing kiosk entries by observed opportunities would give a misleading adoption rate. Keep the existing audit sampling and check its usual coverage across shifts before accepting a result.
- The infection prevention lead validates the audit, decides when to pause rewards, and corrects audit scoring errors. The kiosk owner fixes entry or reporting errors. Before launch, walk through a duplicate, an undo, an outage, and a moment recorded the old way. An audit with inadequate coverage cannot trigger a payout. A valid audit five or more points below baseline pauses the reward pending review.

**Give staff feedback they can use.** The kiosk confirms privately that an entry was saved. At existing huddles, share the ward’s latest audit result, target, and practical fixes. Managers see ward totals only. Keep individual entries out of programme reports and performance reviews. Staff can pick up with the next appropriate moment after time away.

**Stagger the rewards and end them on schedule.** In month 1, give all three wards the fixes, paid practice, and feedback. Start the two-month reward windows in months 2, 3, and 4, one ward at a time. Compare changes in wards with active rewards with changes in wards still waiting. With three wards and monthly observations, treat the comparison as directional. Before each window closes, make the kiosk’s usual entry screen the default when it opens and put access where nurses already do the work. End each ward’s reward after its window and check the first valid monthly audit afterwards, roughly four weeks later.

Use one decision measure per ward: **the post-reward audit rate minus the ward’s target**. At target or above, keep the workflow and feedback. Up to five points below target, review barriers with nurses and adjust the workflow. Five or more points below target, drop the reward design from future rollouts and have infection prevention review the decline. Use staff feedback and existing kiosk totals to assess whether the kiosk itself is being used and helps the work.

**Watch for two misleading signals.** More kiosk entries can reflect duplicate or rushed recording. Better audit scores can reflect staff reacting to observers or a change in which shifts were sampled. Discuss discrepancies with staff at huddles and route safety concerns through the ward’s existing process. One coordinator can run the programme in under an hour a week using the existing audit report and huddles.
