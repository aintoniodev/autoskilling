# Review Within a Day: an eight-week pilot

**Promise to authors:** Get a useful first review within one working day. That means a decision or a concrete next step, not necessarily a merge. The 25-person team is currently at a 2.6-day median; the pilot’s finish line is **90% answered within one working day in weeks 7–8**, without worse review quality.

### What earns credit

One provisional credit per pull request (PR), awarded to the engineer who sends the **first substantive review on time**. A substantive review is an approval that names what was checked, a request for changes with actionable feedback, or a specific blocking question that explains what the author needs to clarify. “Looking now,” reaction emojis, bot messages, self-reviews, and duplicate review rounds earn nothing. A late review still helps the author; it just doesn’t earn credit.

Each credit gets a quiet, visible “Review answered on time” receipt in the reviewer’s private view. No cash, swag, or public points. If the team meets the speed and quality goals, celebrate at the final retro and keep the review coverage that made it possible.

### How it’s measured

The engineering manager publishes one versioned policy before launch: dates, eligible repos, working calendar, scoring and exception rules, workload brackets, targets, visibility, recognition, and audit rules. Backfill the previous four weeks from the code host using the same definitions. Publish the baseline on-time rate and verify whether the 2.6-day median holds under those definitions.

The clock starts when a non-draft PR enters the review queue. One working day is eight hours on the published team calendar, excluding weekends and holidays. A named primary and backup cover each queue; leave changes coverage, not the clock. Reassignment, switching back to draft, and reopening don’t restart it. Recompute from the code host every night. For the weekly rate, count PRs whose deadline fell that week, including unanswered and unassigned PRs as misses. Report genuine withdrawals separately with a reason; audit them for abuse.

Each reviewer’s goal is to answer **their assigned work**, not reach a fixed count. Set capacity before each week based on availability, on-call duty, and expected complexity. A streak advances when all reviews due that week are answered on time. A week with nothing due freezes it. Flag a blocker before the deadline so the backup can act; an accepted exception protects the streak but does not advance it by itself or erase a team miss. Publish what changed because of blocker reports. Six qualifying weeks earn a private graduation note.

### What people see

The team sees the current queue and deadlines, aggregate on-time rate with counts, median wait, overdue backlog, and quality trend. Queues starting below 60% get an interim line of **10 percentage points above baseline by week 4**, not a lower final standard. Individuals see their own receipts and streaks, plus optional private milestones for first review, repeat use, regularity, on-time work, reviewing across areas, fixing a blocker, or coaching a peer.

**No leaderboard at launch.** If the sponsor enables one, show only the top five opt-ins per comparable workload bracket, hide brackets with fewer than eight opt-ins, and let anyone remove their card. At launch, tell engineers that managers can see everyone’s review activity and exceptions for coverage and coaching, not performance ratings.

### What could go wrong, and how it ends

- **Rubber stamps or easy-PR hunting:** Assign work from the oldest eligible queue, matching expertise and capacity. Each week a rotating engineer audits five randomly selected credited reviews for substance and checks for split PRs, reciprocal approvals, or suspicious withdrawals.
- **Faster reviews, more rework:** Track review-related reopens or rollbacks within seven days of merge, per 100 merged PRs. If credits rise and that rate exceeds baseline by five percentage points, pause recognition and inspect the cases. Publish counts as well as rates so a small denominator doesn’t masquerade as a trend.
- **Interruptions and overload:** Protect a daily review slot and have the backup take over when capacity is exhausted. Don’t expect after-hours responses or reward raw volume.

At the end of week 8, stop enrolling PRs and let existing deadlines run out. Close the pilot after the last in-scope deadline and a seven-day quality follow-up. Reconcile provisional credits, give a reason for each reversal and a chance to contest it, then publish the final speed and quality results. Retire credits, badges, streaks, and any leaderboard regardless of outcome. Keep the queue and coverage routine if they helped; if speed improved at the expense of quality, fix the review process rather than extending the contest.
