# One working day: an eight-week code review pilot

**Goal:** Get useful reviews back to authors sooner without lowering the review bar. The reported median is 2.6 days. Before launch, recalculate it and the share reviewed within one working day using the same rules as the pilot. The finish line is **90% on time across weeks 7–8**, with no unresolved quality alarm. If fewer than half of baseline PRs were on time, use 65% by week 4 as an interim milestone, not a lower final standard.

### What earns credit

One provisional credit per PR goes to the reviewer who delivers the first **substantive, on-time review** to the author. That means an approval with a brief note on what was checked, or a request for changes with a specific issue and a clear next step. Acknowledgements, emoji, bot reviews, self-reviews, and second passes earn nothing. The deadline is for useful feedback, not approval or merge.

Assign one primary reviewer when a PR is marked ready. A rotating backup covers leave, incidents, and overloaded queues. Give reviewers protected time during the workday; do not reward after-hours work or raw volume.

### How it’s measured

- **Start the clock when the PR is marked ready**, even if routing is late. One working day is eight hours on the published team calendar, excluding nights, weekends, and team holidays. Reassignment or switching back to draft does not reset the clock.
- **Use the PR system as the record.** Recompute every night from readiness, assignment, review, and closure events, plus logged exceptions. Group PRs by the week their deadline falls. Open overdue PRs count as misses. Report genuine withdrawals separately and audit them; merging without a qualifying review counts as a miss.
- **Measure people against work assigned, not against one another.** A reviewer earns an on-time week by answering at least 90% of their eligible assignments due that week, rounded up. No work due freezes the streak. A genuine blocker logged before the deadline and before handing off or falling back excuses that assignment; if nothing remains due, the week freezes. The team clock keeps running. Publish what changed because of blocker reports in the weekly update.

Before kickoff, publish one versioned policy object with the dates, calendar, scope, credit rules, thresholds, exceptions, visibility, recognition, quality triggers, and audit process. Don’t move the finish line mid-pilot.

### What people see

Everyone sees the team’s on-time count and denominator, median and 90th-percentile wait, overdue queue, and quality trend. Each reviewer privately sees their own deadlines, streak, and a quiet confirmation for every credit: “Review delivered on time,” linked to the PR. No channel spam.

The sponsor keeps the leaderboard **off** for this pilot. If reconsidered later, require opt-in, show only the top five within comparable assignment-volume brackets, hide brackets with fewer than eight opt-ins, and let anyone hide their card. Say at launch that engineering managers can see everyone’s pilot review activity, including people who opt out of recognition, and that it won’t be used in performance ratings.

Offer optional private badges for first review, repeat, regular reviewing, on-time streak, reviewing across components, fixing a blocker, and coaching a peer. Badges carry no extra credit. No individual prizes; if the team hits the target, it chooses one review-friction fix to get protected engineering time.

### What could go wrong, and how it ends

**Rubber-stamping, easy-PR cherry-picking, and hidden waits.** Route by ownership and available capacity, with oldest due first among suitable reviewers. Each week, a rotating senior engineer audits five credited reviews, including higher-risk PRs. Compare the share of merged PRs needing review-related rework or reverts within seven days against baseline. If credits per eligible PR rise and that rate rises by five percentage points or more, or an audit finds a rubber stamp on a high-risk change, pause recognition and investigate. If review demand routinely exceeds capacity, rebalance assignments rather than asking people to work faster.

**Sunset.** At week 8, stop enrolling PRs and stop streaks. Once the last deadlines and seven-day quality window have passed, audit flagged credits, reverse gamed credits with a written reason and a chance to correct the record, then finalize. Privately recognize reviewers with five consecutive on-time active weeks as graduates; frozen weeks neither add to nor break the streak. Publish the team result and retire credits, badges, and individual score views. If speed improved without a quality cost, keep the owner, backup, and team-level monitoring. Otherwise, publish what failed and fix the queue before trying another incentive.
