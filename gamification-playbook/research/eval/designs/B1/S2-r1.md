# One Working Day: an eight-week code review pilot

**Goal:** Get at least 90% of review-ready pull requests a useful first review within one working day. The reported median is 2.6 days. Before launch, rescore the previous four weeks using the pilot rules so the comparison is fair. Present this as a test of review turnaround, not a contest or a performance rating.

### What earns credit

The author gets a review they can act on: an approval after inspection, or feedback that names a specific issue and a next step. The first reviewer to deliver that within eight staffed working hours earns **one provisional credit per PR**. Acknowledgements, emoji, bot comments, self-reviews and duplicate reviews earn nothing. A substantive review that requests changes counts just as much as an approval. Later review rounds remain part of the job but earn no extra credit.

The clock starts when a non-draft PR first enters the review queue. Nights, weekends and published team holidays do not count. A rotating triage owner assigns a primary reviewer and backup, routes around leave and watches the oldest requests. Reassignment, reopening or switching back to draft does not reset the clock. If coverage is thin, the manager reallocates work rather than asking people to review after hours.

### How it’s measured

The engineering manager publishes one versioned pilot policy before kickoff: dates, working calendar, eligibility, credit rules, workload brackets, streak exceptions, visibility, targets, reward, quality thresholds and appeals. Recalculate nightly from the PR system and its linked blocker and incident records.

The headline measure is **PRs answered on time ÷ eligible PRs whose deadline fell that week**. Unanswered PRs count as misses. Show counts alongside percentages, plus median first-response time and the oldest unanswered PRs. The team finish line is 90% across weeks 5–8. Ownership groups starting below 60% get an interim week-four target of 10 percentage points over baseline, without changing the one-day SLA. If the team meets the finish line without an unresolved quality alarm, it gets a protected two-hour block to fix a review bottleneck of its choice.

### What people see

- **Everyone:** A team scoreboard and the live review queue. Each provisional credit gets a quiet “reviewed on time” mark on the PR and moves the team counter. No individual leaderboard, public streaks or celebratory chat spam.
- **Each engineer:** Their own due reviews, credits, streak and optional private milestones. Set milestones as a share of assigned work, not a raw total; if the sponsor later enables rankings, compare only within similar workload brackets. Rankings are opt-in, top five only, hidden below eight opted-in people per bracket, and anyone can hide their card. Private milestones recognize first and repeat reviews, regular on-time work, reviewing across areas, fixing a blocker and coaching a peer.
- **Managers:** Everyone’s activity, including exceptions and reversals. Disclose this at kickoff and commit not to use pilot data for performance ratings.

A streak advances when all of an engineer’s non-excused reviews due that week get substantive answers on time. A week with nothing due, or only excused reviews, freezes it. To qualify for an excuse, flag a genuine blocker before the deadline and before falling back, so triage can help. An excuse protects the individual streak, not the team metric. Each week, publish what changed because someone raised a blocker.

### What could go wrong, and how it ends

**Rubber stamps:** Each week, a rotating engineer samples five credited reviews across PR sizes. Track review-related reverts and avoidable rework per merged PR against baseline. If credits per week rise while either quality rate worsens in a rolling two-week window, flag it and inspect the cases before awarding the team reward. Pause recognition if the cases show rushed reviews. Don’t treat ordinary changes requested *during* review as rework.

**Cherry-picking, overload or hiding work:** Route by age, ownership and capacity, not ease. Check misses by PR size and workload bracket, and audit unusual draft toggles, withdrawals and reciprocal approvals. Keep legitimate withdrawals visible in a separate count so removing PRs cannot silently improve the score.

**At week eight:** Stop taking new entries and let in-flight deadlines settle. In the following week, the manager and an engineer audit flagged credits, reverse invalid ones with a written reason and give reviewers two working days to appeal before finalizing. Recognize engineers with six qualifying weeks in an unbroken streak privately. Publish the final speed and quality results, retire credits and milestones, and keep the review rotation and queue reporting only if they helped.
