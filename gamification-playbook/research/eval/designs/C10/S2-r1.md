## What engineers hear at launch

Today, the median wait for a code review is 2.6 days. We’re aiming to give authors an answer within one working day so they can keep moving. Before the pilot, we’ll use a paid team session to identify what slows reviews down and confirm whether a paid hour back is a reward people would value. We’ll publish the fixes you asked for and give the on-duty reviewer protected time each day to clear the queue.

**A review counts once per ready PR when an assigned reviewer submits a substantive answer within eight working hours.** An approval after review, a change request that explains what needs fixing, or a justified hold that names the blocker and who can resolve it all count. Acknowledgements and assignment changes leave the clock running.

When your review counts, you’ll get a private confirmation on the PR. You’ll see your own progress against a goal of answering four out of five assigned reviews on time, based on the work you actually receive. Leave and off-duty time are covered by the rota. If you miss a review, pick up the next one. The team will see aggregate progress in the channel we already use for engineering updates. Managers see those same totals, and the counts stay out of performance reviews.

Each pilot group has four weeks to answer at least 80% of its eligible PRs on time. If your group reaches that milestone, everyone in it gets **one paid hour off**. The engineering lead will announce it at the team meeting and make sure coverage is arranged so everyone can take it. One group starts earning the reward two weeks after the other. Both groups get the queue improvements and protected review time from day one.

## How it runs

- **Make the queue work first.** Use the existing PR queue, assign a primary and backup reviewer, and put the oldest ready requests first. In the paid session, ask engineers which requests need specialist coverage and what interrupts review time. Give everyone two weeks with the fixes before the first reward window opens. Keep the rota, protected time and queue order in place after week six.
- **Measure the wait authors experience.** Use the repository’s ready-for-review, assignment and submitted-review timestamps. Start the clock when a PR is ready and review is requested, even if assignment is late. Count eight hours on the published team working calendar, excluding weekends and holidays. Route out-of-office assignments to the backup without resetting the clock. Include unanswered PRs once their deadline passes. The engineering lead corrects bad counts from the PR record and runs the pilot in under an hour a week.
- **Watch for rubber stamps, easy-PR picking and misuse of holds.** Assign from the queue and count each PR only once, even if it’s reopened or split into review rounds. Use the team’s existing defect triage to look for review misses. Pause rewards if triage confirms two PRs in a week were approved without a substantive review, or one such approval caused a production incident. Keep individual counts private and use the team update to surface coverage problems.
- **Decide whether the improvement lasts.** Before launch, record the share of eligible PRs answered within one working day using the same definition. Randomly choose which of two comparable review groups starts earning first, then compare their on-time shares during the two-week stagger. Treat that comparison as directional with 25 engineers. Four weeks after the last reward ends, use the team’s on-time share to decide: **keep** the workflow at 80% or higher, **change** it if the rate is below 80% but above baseline, and **drop** the pilot setup if it’s at or below baseline. The reward ends at week six.
