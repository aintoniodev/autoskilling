# One-working-day code reviews

**Fix the review queue first.** Ask five engineers across workstreams what makes reviews sit and which reward they’d actually want. Give them 30 minutes of paid work time to try the new flow. Put ready PRs in the existing review queue, oldest first, with an owner and a backup. Protect a daily review block and have the backup pick up requests when the owner is away. Show the team what changed in response to their feedback.

**Reward the first review that lets the author act.** The rule at launch is “A ready PR counts once when a human reviewer submits a substantive review within one working day.” A review can approve after checking the change, request specific changes, or give actionable feedback that lets the author proceed. An acknowledgment, emoji, or bot comment earns no credit. A complex PR can get a timely review that flags a specific risk and says what needs checking next. Approval and merge have no deadline under this programme.

**Use the events the Git host already records.** Start the clock when a human-authored PR first becomes ready for review. Count eight hours within an agreed team schedule, excluding weekends and team holidays. Measure the percentage of eligible PRs that receive an on-time review, including overdue and unanswered PRs in the denominator once their deadline passes. Exclude draft-only and bot-authored PRs. Reassignment, a return to draft, reopening, and repeat reviews of the same PR earn no extra credit and never restart the clock. A substantive review submitted through the usual Git interface counts regardless of how the reviewer found the PR. Before launch, test the count against a duplicate review, a reopened PR, an unassigned PR, and a Friday request.

**Keep feedback private and make the reward shared.** After a review counts, send the reviewer a private confirmation through the existing review workflow. Engineers can see their own assigned requests and aim to answer four of every five on time. For someone with fewer than five requests, the goal is simply to answer the next one on time. A missed week carries no penalty. Post one weekly team total in the engineering channel. Managers get the same total, with no individual drill-down or use in performance reviews. Announce the proposed reward at launch, after checking it with engineers. If the team reaches 80% on time over the final two reward weeks and clears the quality gate, everyone earns two hours of paid time off, with coverage arranged by the lead. The lead announces it in person at the team meeting.

**Run for nine weeks.** In weeks 1–2, give everyone the workflow fixes and private feedback. Measure the baseline using the same one-day rule. The current 2.6-day median gives context for the problem. In weeks 3–4, announce and open reward eligibility for one existing review group, then open it for the other in weeks 5–6. Both groups qualify together based on weeks 5–6. Use the stagger only if the groups already have mostly separate review queues. If reviewers are shared, start the reward for everyone in week 3 and treat the before-and-after comparison as directional. End rewards after week 6. Keep the default queue, backup routing, and protected review time. Check the on-time rate again in weeks 8–9. Keep the workflow at 80% or above, adjust routing or capacity at 65–79%, and retire the incentive format below 65%.

**Watch for rubber stamps and uneven workload.** The review rotation lead samples five credited reviews each week using existing PR access. Pause rewards if two or more of the five are superficial, then correct the counts and adjust the rule or workload before resuming. The rotation lead decides disputed cases and corrects the tally. During the existing team meeting, ask whether harder PRs are getting left behind, authors are delaying the ready flag, or the same people are covering the queue. Use those reports to fix routing. Automate the tally from existing events so the sample, corrections, and weekly update fit within 45 minutes.
