## One working day for a useful review

**Fix the queue first.** Ask five engineers who regularly open and review PRs what causes the delay. Give them 30 minutes of protected work time to walk through recent examples. Use the first two weeks to fix the biggest friction point and show the team what changed. Start with a rotating primary reviewer and backup, a protected daily review slot, and ready PRs ordered oldest first in the team’s existing workflow. Route requests around planned absences.

**Reward a useful first review.** Announce at launch that everyone on the 25-person team earns two hours of paid time off if at least 80% of eligible PRs get a substantive first review within one working day over the four-week reward period. Agree coverage with the engineering manager before announcing it. Confirm the award at the team meeting and let people take it over the following month. There are no individual prizes, rankings, or streaks. After an absence or a miss, the next PR is a fresh chance to contribute.

**Count one outcome per PR.** Use existing pull-request and review events. The clock starts when a human-authored PR first leaves draft and has a review request. One working day means eight hours on the team’s published working calendar, excluding weekends and holidays. A submitted approval, a specific request for changes, or a review that identifies a concrete blocker and the next step counts. An emoji, “looking soon,” or a bare acknowledgment does not. Unanswered PRs count as misses once their deadlines pass. Attribute results to the week the request started and wait for those deadlines before closing the tally.

Before launch, replay the rule on recent examples. A duplicate review adds zero. Redrafting or reassigning the same PR does not restart the clock. A ready PR merged without review counts as a miss. Exclude bot PRs and documented emergency bypasses, and show their weekly counts so changes in exclusions are visible. The rotation lead fixes disputed counts from the PR history and records the reason.

**Make feedback immediate and private.** Send reviewers a private confirmation when a submitted review provisionally counts, with their own progress toward a goal sized to the review slots they’re scheduled to cover. Post one weekly update in the existing team channel with the team’s on-time rate, numerator and denominator, and progress toward the reward. Managers get the same aggregate, with no per-person drill-down. Programme data stays out of performance reviews.

**Run it for ten weeks.** In weeks 1–2, introduce the queue fixes and feedback for everyone and measure the on-time rate. In weeks 3–4, start the reward for one existing review rotation, chosen by a coin flip, while the other continues with the same fixes and feedback. In weeks 5–6, both rotations are eligible, and in weeks 7–8, the second rotation finishes its four-week reward window. Apply the same team reward rule within each rotation’s window so everyone has four eligible weeks. Use the stagger as a rough check on what the reward adds, since the same engineers may review across rotations. Keep the queue, backup and protected review time in place. In weeks 9–10, measure again with prizes off using the same on-time rate. **Keep** the workflow as is at 80% or higher, **change** the workflow if it improves on weeks 1–2 but falls short of 80%, and **drop** the incentive format if performance returns to or below that baseline.

**Watch for rubber stamps and cherry-picking.** Each week, the rotation lead samples five counted reviews across PR sizes, including reviews submitted just before the deadline. If two fail the substantive-review rule, the engineering manager pauses reward accrual, the lead corrects the counts, and the manager resumes accrual after the next sample passes. The lead also checks the oldest overdue requests for large PRs that keep getting skipped or work concentrated on the same reviewers, then uses the backup or rebalances the rotation. Budget 45 minutes a week for the sample, corrections and team update.
