# Hand-hygiene kiosk pilot

**Reward a ward for hand hygiene that is both performed and accurately logged.** Offer every nurse on a ward that reaches its target a 30-minute paid break with relief coverage. Announce it at launch, have the ward lead thank the team at a huddle, and offer slots across shifts. Eligibility is team-wide, including nurses who were on leave. Each ward works toward its own target.

**Fix the workflow before starting.** Walk through the kiosk with nurses from day and night shifts on all three wards. Test placement, availability, and the time it takes to log during a busy shift. Give nurses protected time to practice and show them what changed based on their feedback. Agree with infection prevention when logging can safely happen. Patient care takes priority, and an unavailable kiosk is reported as a workflow problem.

**Use the existing monthly audit.** For each routinely audited hand-hygiene opportunity, the observer keeps the usual compliance tally and adds one anonymous tick if they can also verify that the completed action was logged through the normal workflow. Count each opportunity once. The ward score is the number of audited opportunities with both completed hygiene and a verified kiosk entry, divided by all audited opportunities in the agreed sample. Use the same mix of shifts and observation approach before, during, and after rewards. Verify entries only where observers can see the normal confirmation during routine observation, without following staff or linking individual records. Confirm that this is feasible during the baseline month. If it is infeasible, fix the verification workflow before offering a logging incentive.

Before launch, test duplicate taps, a corrected entry, hygiene completed without a kiosk entry, and a kiosk outage. A duplicate adds zero credit, a corrected entry counts once if valid, and hygiene without an entry still counts in the usual compliance audit. Record outages separately and hold the reward decision if they make the sample unrepresentative. The infection-prevention lead resolves disputed audit counts and pauses rewards if ordinary observed hygiene compliance falls five percentage points or more below the ward’s baseline. The kiosk owner fixes workflow and confirmation errors.

**Show progress without ranking people.** The kiosk immediately confirms a saved entry and makes corrections easy. At the regular ward huddle, show the latest audited score, sample size, and ward target. Nurses get confirmation for their own entries. Managers receive ward totals only, and pilot data stays out of performance reviews. Every new opportunity is a fresh chance to contribute.

**Run eight weeks of rewards per ward.** Give all three wards the fixes, practice, and feedback first, then collect one monthly baseline audit. Start the reward on one ward at a time, four weeks apart, with the order drawn in advance. Set each ward’s target at 10 percentage points above its baseline score, capped at 95%. Wards already at or above 95% aim to maintain their baseline. Award once if the ward reaches its target across the two reward-period audits, using at least 30 audited opportunities in total and meeting the hygiene safeguard. The stagger gives an early read on what the reward adds beyond the shared improvements. Three wards provide limited evidence, so report the actual counts alongside the percentages.

**End the prize and keep the easy workflow.** Before each ward’s reward window closes, make hand-hygiene logging the kiosk’s first action and keep confirmation and correction available. Four weeks later, use the same audited score to decide what stays. Keep the workflow and routine feedback if the ward still meets its target. If it is above baseline but below target, or the sample is too small, fix the friction and reassess at the next routine audit. If the score returns to baseline or lower in an adequate sample, drop the prize model and review the logging workflow. Keep the ordinary hand-hygiene audit throughout.

The main failure modes are extra taps without hygiene, better behavior only while observers are present, and teams losing a fair chance because of kiosk access or shift coverage. Counting verified opportunities limits the first. Keeping the regular audit approach comparable helps with the second, although the results describe observed practice. Reviewing outages and shift coverage before each reward decision addresses the third. A single coordinator can maintain the ward totals and prepare the huddle update in under an hour a week outside the existing audits.
