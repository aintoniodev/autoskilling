## One working day for a useful review

Run a six-week pilot. In the first week, ask a few engineers who regularly author and review PRs to walk through recent slow reviews. Fix the biggest friction they identify, such as unclear ownership or review requests buried in chat, and tell the team what changed. Put a rotating first reviewer and backup on each area’s review queue. Give reviewers a protected 30-minute block each working day and make the queue show the oldest requests first.

### What earns the reward

Tell engineers the rule in one sentence. “A PR counts once when a teammate submits a useful approval or asks for specific changes within one working day of the PR being ready and review being requested.”

A useful approval gives the author enough context to know what was checked. A request for changes identifies what needs attention. Reviewers can ask for more time when the change warrants it, and authors can act on a request for changes without waiting for final approval. Each review group earns a team lunch if at least 80% of its eligible PRs receive a qualifying review on time during its reward window. A missed PR or a week away carries no individual penalty.

### How it’s measured and what people see

Use the code host’s existing PR and submitted-review events. Start the clock once the PR is out of draft and has a review request. One working day means eight hours on a published team coverage calendar, excluding weekends and team holidays. The backup covers individual absences. Attribute each PR to the review group that owns it when the clock starts. Count each PR once. Rerequests, assignment changes and reopening the same PR keep the original clock. Bot reviews, self-reviews and acknowledgments do not qualify. Count unanswered PRs as misses once their deadlines pass. If an author withdraws a PR before its deadline, exclude it and check withdrawals in the weekly sample.

Keep the feedback in the existing workflow. After a submitted review, show the reviewer whether it counted and the elapsed working time. The queue shows authors which PRs are waiting and when they’re due. Post the group’s weekly on-time rate and progress toward the lunch in the engineering channel. Individual feedback stays private. Managers receive group totals only, and pilot data stays out of performance reviews.

### How it runs and ends

Give everyone the new queue, protected time and feedback in weeks 1–2. Stagger the lunch across two existing review groups with distinct queues. The first group’s reward window is weeks 3–4 and the second’s is weeks 5–6. Use the same event rule to recalculate the pre-pilot on-time rate from existing history. The reported 2.6-day median gives context, while the on-time rate is the decision metric.

Before the lunches end, make the queue the default place for incoming review requests and put the reviewer rotation on the team calendar. Four weeks after all rewards end, use the team’s on-time rate over those four weeks to decide. At 80% or above, keep the workflow and retire the reward. At 65–79%, fix the main source of misses and recheck. Below 65%, drop the incentive and revisit reviewer capacity.

### What could go wrong

Before launch, replay a duplicate request, a dismissed review, a draft reopened as ready and a PR handled in chat. A dismissed review loses credit unless a later qualifying review met the original deadline. A chat review earns credit when the reviewer records it on the PR before the deadline. The engineering lead owns the decision to pause rewards. The rotation owner handles count corrections and spot-checks five credited reviews a week for rubber stamps or vague requests, plus a small sample of withdrawals and PRs merged without review. If two of the five credited reviews fail the quality check, pause reward credit, correct the affected counts and address the cause before restarting. Use the existing queue and event report so the checks, corrections and weekly update fit within one hour.
