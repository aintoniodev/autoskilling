# One working day for code review

**Make reviews easier to pick up.** Ask five engineers, including frequent PR authors and reviewers, where reviews stall. Give them 30 minutes of protected time to walk through recent examples. Use their findings to fix routing and carve out a daily review block during working hours. Put the oldest unanswered PRs first in the team’s existing queue, assign a primary reviewer and a backup, and route around planned absences. Share what engineers reported and what changed. If reviewers still lack time to pick up the queue, fix capacity before starting rewards.

**Reward a useful first review within one working day.** Tell the team the rule in one sentence. “A PR counts once when a teammate submits a review within eight team working hours that either approves it after inspection or gives the author a concrete change or question to act on.” Use the existing PR review-request and submitted-review events. Start the clock when a human-authored PR is ready and first enters the review queue. Count only hours in the team’s agreed work calendar, skipping evenings, weekends and team holidays. Reassignment and repeat requests keep the original clock. A quick acknowledgment does not stop it.

**Use one team metric.** Measure the percentage of eligible PRs that receive a qualifying first review by their deadline. Report only PRs whose deadline has passed, including overdue PRs still waiting. Exclude drafts, bot PRs and PRs genuinely withdrawn before the deadline. Before launch, replay a duplicate request, an approval dismissed before the deadline, an off-platform review and a Friday afternoon request. A dismissed review needs a valid replacement by the original deadline. An off-platform review counts when the reviewer submits its outcome on the PR by the deadline. Calculate the baseline percentage from the last four weeks of PR history. The current 2.6-day median gives context, and the on-time percentage drives decisions.

**Keep feedback private and the reward shared.** After a qualifying review, send the reviewer private confirmation with the PR link and whether it met the deadline. Show each engineer their own on-time count against the eligible first reviews they picked up, with a personal goal of four out of five across their next five cases. Progress carries across absences, so a missed week simply leaves the next case open. Post the team’s percentage and PR count weekly in the channel the team already uses. Managers receive team totals only. Programme data stays out of performance reviews. If a rotation reaches 80% across its four reward weeks, everyone in it gets one paid team lunch, with a meal allowance for remote staff.

**Run eight weeks, then check again four weeks later.** Give everyone the queue fixes and feedback in weeks 1–2. Use two stable review rotations with comparable PR volume. Start the four-week reward in weeks 3–6 for one rotation and weeks 5–8 for the other. Compare their team-level results during the two-week stagger to get a directional read on what the lunch adds. Keep the queue order, backup routing and review block in place after week 8. Recheck the same on-time percentage across weeks 9–12. Keep the setup at 80% or higher. Below 80%, change it if performance improved over baseline. If it returns to baseline or worse, drop the incentive and revisit where reviews stall.

**Guard against rushed approvals and easy-case picking.** Have the rotation lead sample five submitted reviews each week, including approvals and complex PRs. Pause reward credit if two of the latest ten sampled reviews fail the rule. For approvals, ask the lead to check whether the diff contains an obvious material issue the review missed. The engineering lead decides when rewards resume. The queue owner corrects disputed counts in private and checks withdrawn or repeatedly reassigned PRs for hidden delays. Use the existing queue and PR history so one owner can run the weekly tally, sample and corrections in under an hour.
