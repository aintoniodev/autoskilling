## One working day for a code review

Give authors a useful first review within one working day. Start by asking five engineers across the team what makes reviews hard to pick up and what makes a fast review useful. Give them 30 minutes of protected work time to try the new queue and test the counting rule. Publish the fixes in the team channel under “You reported it, we fixed it.”

Put the oldest ready PRs first in the team’s existing review queue. Assign a primary reviewer and a backup from the existing ownership rotation, accounting for leave. Give reviewers a protected daily review block and let the backup pick up anything the primary cannot cover before it is due. Start rewards once the queue, routing and review time are working.

### What earns credit

**A ready PR counts once when its first peer review gives the author a clear decision or an actionable next step within one working day.**

Use ready-for-review, review-request and submitted-review timestamps already recorded by the repository. Start the clock when the PR is both ready and review is requested. One working day means eight hours on the team’s agreed working calendar, excluding weekends and team holidays. The denominator is all eligible PRs whose deadline fell that week, including those still unanswered. Count reviews submitted through the repository regardless of how the reviewer found the PR.

A considered approval, a request for changes, or a submitted review with a specific blocker and next step qualifies. Acknowledgements and bot reviews earn no credit. Before launch, test the rule against duplicate requests, reassignment, new commits, a dismissed review, a withdrawn PR and a review picked up from the old queue. Duplicate requests, reassignment and new commits leave the original deadline in place. Use the next qualifying review if the first was dismissed before the deadline. Exclude PRs the author withdraws before the deadline and before any qualifying review.

### What people see and earn

Send a private confirmation through the existing workflow after a qualifying review, for example, “PR #412 reviewed in 5 working hours.” Each engineer sees their own on-time share against an 80% personal target, based on the requests assigned to them. With fewer than five requests, show the count without a target verdict. Personal progress carries no prize.

In the existing weekly team update, show the team’s on-time share and the underlying count, such as 18 of 22. Managers see team totals only. Keep individual data out of performance reviews. During each reward window, two weeks at or above 80% earn that group a 60-minute paid learning block per person. The weeks can be nonconsecutive, and everyone in the group gets the time. A missed week leaves the next one open.

### Run and ending

Use two existing ownership groups, with each PR attributed to its original group even if a backup from the other group reviews it. In weeks 1–2, give both groups the queue changes and feedback. Start the four-week reward window for one group in week 3 and the other in week 5. Compare their on-time shares during weeks 3–4 to see whether rewards appear to add anything, keeping in mind that a few PRs can move either group’s rate.

Before week 8 ends, make the oldest-ready queue and backup routing the permanent defaults. Keep the protected review block and private feedback. Four weeks after the final reward window, use the **team-wide share of eligible PRs reviewed within one working day** over those four weeks to decide what to keep. Set the thresholds before launch. At 80% or higher, keep the workflow. Below 80% but above the pre-launch share, adjust the workflow. At or below the pre-launch share, retire the campaign and revisit how review work is staffed. Compute the baseline from the prior four weeks using the same rule. The current 2.6-day median gives context.

### What could go wrong

- **Rubber stamps or cherry-picking.** The review lead samples five credited reviews each week across both groups, including complex PRs when available. Pause rewards if two or more lack evidence of a meaningful review. The lead makes the pause and restart decision. The repository maintainer fixes disputed counts and tells the affected engineer privately.
- **Review work piles up on a few people.** The lead checks the oldest requests during the existing team check-in and shifts coverage when someone is overloaded. Hard PRs stay eligible for timely, actionable feedback even when final approval needs longer.
- **The programme feels like surveillance.** Use existing events, keep confirmations private, and skip leaderboards and streaks. Budget 45 minutes a week for sampling, count corrections and the team update.
