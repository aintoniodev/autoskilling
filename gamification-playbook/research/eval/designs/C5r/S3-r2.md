## Three-ward hand-hygiene pilot

**Reward the ward for observed hand hygiene.** At launch, announce that each ward can earn a hospital-funded meal, offered across all shifts, and a personal thank-you from the chief nursing officer. A ward earns it if its two monthly audits during the reward period, pooled by opportunity, meet the hospital’s existing compliance target or improve by at least 5 percentage points over its own baseline. Each ward can win. Nights, part-time staff and staff who miss a week share in the reward. Kiosk entries earn zero points.

**Make logging quick enough to fit the shift.** Before launch, give nurses from day and night shifts 15 paid minutes to try the kiosk during a normal workflow and identify delays. Fix kiosk placement, slow sign-in and duplicate paperwork before starting the pilot. Make the entry screen the default, prefill the ward and keep entries free of patient identifiers. The rule nurses see is “Log a completed hand-hygiene action once, when patient care allows.” During urgent care or kiosk downtime, staff continue normal hand hygiene and resume logging at the next suitable moment. Share what changed in response to staff feedback at the next huddle.

**Use the monthly audit for compliance and sample kiosk adoption separately.** Infection Prevention calculates compliance as observed actions divided by observed opportunities under the existing audit protocol. Keep audit timing unannounced and use a comparable mix of shifts across periods. To estimate adoption, match completed actions from a small subset of existing audit windows to kiosk entries by ward, time and moment type where those fields already exist. Use each entry at most once, count ambiguous matches as unverified and report the sample size alongside the verified logging percentage. Set a minimum usable sample before launch. Avoid collecting staff identifiers or adding a new observation round. Direct observation can still be affected by who is observed and how people behave around observers. [WHO hand-hygiene monitoring guidance](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK144028/)

**Show staff immediate confirmation and team progress.** The kiosk confirms “Hand-hygiene entry saved” and offers a short undo window. The existing ward huddle board shows the latest audited compliance, the ward’s goal and the sampled logging percentage. Managers see the same ward-level results. Individual activity stays out of manager dashboards, rankings and performance reviews. The ward educator takes private reports of incorrect entries, and the kiosk administrator fixes confirmed counting errors.

**Stagger the reward and set the end date at launch.** Give all three wards the kiosk fixes, training and feedback during a two-month baseline. Randomly assign wards to start rewards in months 3, 4 and 5. Each ward gets two reward months, then keeps the kiosk defaults and feedback. Use the stagger to see whether improvement starts with the workflow changes or around reward launch. At each ward’s first monthly audit after rewards end, use the **verified logging percentage** to decide the kiosk’s future. Keep it at 80% or above, revise the workflow at 60–79%, and drop routine kiosk logging below 60%. Defer the decision to the next audit if the sample is too small.

**Pause if care or data quality suffers.** Before launch, test a double tap, an undone entry, an action completed without logging and a kiosk outage. Infection Prevention pauses reward decisions if audited compliance falls 5 percentage points below the ward’s baseline or staff report a credible care delay tied to logging. A surge in entries without a corresponding rise in verified logging triggers a check for duplicates, backfilling or recording errors. The ward educator runs the pilot in under an hour a week using the existing huddle and audit reports.
