## One working day for a useful review

Run a six-week team pilot. The target is for at least 90% of review requests to receive a useful first response within one working day. The reported median is 2.6 days. Recalculate it using the pilot’s clock so the team can compare like for like.

### What is rewarded

If the team meets the target in each of the final two weeks and passes the quality and workload checks, everyone gets two protected hours of work time to spend on an engineering improvement they choose. The engineering manager commits to that time before launch and schedules it within the following two weeks. The reward is shared across all 25 engineers, including people who contribute through specialist reviews, backup coverage, or work that leaves them with fewer review opportunities.

Each workday, a rotating pair checks the review queue at the start and midpoint of the team’s working day. They route unowned requests and arrange backup when a reviewer is unavailable. The assigned reviewer still owns the response. If the pair cannot find coverage, the manager reprioritizes work. Review time comes out of planned engineering capacity.

### How it is measured

- **Start the clock** when a pull request is ready for review and a review is requested. Count eight working hours on one published team calendar, excluding weekends and team holidays. Reassignment leaves the original deadline in place. After a substantive review, an explicitly requested re-review starts a new cycle.
- **Stop the clock** when a non-author submits a review that approves the change, requests changes with specific feedback, or raises a concrete question needed to proceed. An acknowledgement, emoji, or promise to review later leaves the clock running. A pull request can need more than a day to reach approval.
- **Count every eligible request** in the week its deadline falls, including overdue requests still awaiting a response. Exclude drafts and bot-only requests. Log withdrawals with a reason. A request withdrawn after its deadline still counts as a miss if it had no qualifying response.
- **Check quality and workload.** Each week, an engineer outside that week’s coverage pair and the manager audit ten randomly sampled responses, or all responses if fewer than ten. They check whether each response shows evidence of review and gives the author a clear next step or a justified approval. Passing requires at least 90% of sampled responses to meet that bar. An anonymous weekly pulse asks whether anyone reviewed outside normal hours to meet the target or rushed a review they would ordinarily have taken longer on. Any report triggers a workload adjustment. A week with a reported instance cannot qualify for the reward.

### What people see

Keep one shared queue with each pull request’s owner, deadline, and current state. Send a private reminder to the owner and coverage pair two working hours before the deadline. In the existing weekly engineering update, show the team’s on-time count and percentage, median and 90th-percentile response times, audit results, and the main reasons for misses. Keep the reporting at team level and out of performance reviews.

### How it runs and ends

In week one, publish the rules, collect the baseline from the previous four weeks, and check the queue against real pull requests. In weeks two through four, run the rotation and fix routing or capacity problems at the weekly review. Weeks five and six determine the reward. Require at least ten eligible requests in each qualifying week. If volume is lower, mark the result inconclusive and end on schedule.

At the end of week six, publish the result and end the reward. If the team qualifies, schedule the protected time. In the retrospective, decide whether the queue and rotation earned a place in normal work by looking at response times, review quality, and the cost of coverage.

### What could go wrong

- **Rubber-stamping or token comments.** Review the actual feedback in the audit and correct any misclassified responses before calculating the result.
- **Clock manipulation.** Track withdrawals, returns to draft, and reassignments. Review unusual patterns in the weekly check and preserve the original deadline when a request is reopened without a material change.
- **Specialists become the bottleneck.** Track misses caused by missing expertise and schedule backup coverage. Give specialist reviews explicit space in sprint planning.
- **The target encourages interruptions.** Let the coverage pair handle routing in two planned windows. If the workload pulse flags rushing or after-hours work, cut or defer planned work before adding reminders or asking people to pick up more reviews.
