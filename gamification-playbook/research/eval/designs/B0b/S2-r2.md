## One working day for a useful first review

Run an eight-week pilot for the 25-engineer team. The commitment is a useful first review within one working day. Authors should know what they can do next, whether that means proceeding, making a change, or answering a specific question.

### What earns the reward

The team earns one protected half-day for engineer-chosen maintenance or tooling if at least **90% of eligible PRs receive a useful first review within eight working hours** across the final four pilot weeks and the quality and workload checks hold. The engineering manager books the time in the next sprint and reduces planned work to make room. There are no individual scores, prizes, or rankings.

Each working day, a rotating reviewer triages incoming PRs during two scheduled checks. A named backup covers absences and takes overflow. The manager budgets both engineers’ review time in sprint planning. The reviewer routes specialist work early and can ask the manager to reprioritize when demand exceeds capacity.

### How it’s measured

- **Start the clock** when a human-authored PR is ready for review and a review is requested from the team. Track one clock per PR. Reassignments and draft toggles after the first request leave the original start time in place. Count eight hours on a published team working calendar, excluding evenings, weekends, and team holidays. The backup covers individual leave.
- **Stop the clock** at the first substantive review by an eligible reviewer. An informed approval, a specific change request, or a question that identifies a concrete issue and gives the author a next step qualifies. Acknowledgments, bot comments, and reassignment messages leave the clock running.
- **Count misses** once eight working hours elapse, even if the PR is still open. Group PRs by request date and finalize results after each PR has had its full window. Show withdrawals separately. A withdrawal after the deadline still counts as a miss. Track median and 90th-percentile response times alongside the on-time rate.
- **Check quality and workload weekly.** The engineering lead samples five responses, mixing quick approvals and other review outcomes, and asks authors privately whether the response let them act. If two or more fail the usefulness check, expand the audit and correct any misclassified responses before scoring. Track missed planned work and after-hours reviews attributable to the rotation. The reward requires any recurring quality or workload issue to be resolved by the end of the pilot.

### What people see

In the existing PR queue, engineers see the assigned reviewer, age in working hours, and upcoming deadline. A weekly team update shows the on-time rate with its numerator and denominator, median and 90th-percentile times, overdue PRs, and any capacity issue that needs action. Share audit themes in aggregate. Discuss the results in the existing retro.

### How it runs and ends

Use weeks 1–2 to validate the clock and establish a comparable baseline. The reported median is 2.6 days, so recalculate it using the same working-hours definition as the pilot. Start the rotation in week 3, use weeks 3–4 to fix routing and coverage, and score weeks 5–8. At the final retro, publish the result, award the half-day if earned, and retire the incentive and weekly scorecard. Keep the rotation and deadline view if they improved response times at a sustainable cost. Check the on-time rate once more 30 days later.

### What could go wrong

- **Rubber stamps or token comments.** Use the audit to remove invalid responses from the count and discuss examples of useful reviews in retro.
- **Easy PRs get picked first.** Triage by deadline and route specialist PRs immediately. Use the 90th percentile and overdue queue to spot work that keeps waiting.
- **The review burden lands on the same people.** Rotate primary and backup duty, budget it explicitly, and have the manager shift planned work when the queue exceeds capacity.
- **The first response is fast, but the author stays blocked.** Inspect long gaps after an author replies in the weekly sample. Assign the next action to a named person in the PR.
