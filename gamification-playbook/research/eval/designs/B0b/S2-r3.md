## One-working-day review pilot

Run a six-week pilot for the 25-engineer team. Aim for **at least 90% of review-ready pull requests to get a substantive human response within eight working hours**. A first response gives the author a clear next step. The reviewer can approve, request changes, or identify a specific blocker that prevents review.

### What earns the reward

If the team meets the target in at least three of the final four weeks, including the last week, and at least 90% of sampled responses pass the quality check, everyone gets a protected two-hour block the following sprint to fix a developer-workflow problem of their choice. The engineering manager removes equivalent planned work from the sprint. Individual review counts, response speed, and approvals carry no reward.

Rotate a primary reviewer and a backup each working day, accounting for leave and code ownership. The primary gets 45 minutes of protected review time and checks the queue early enough to route specialist requests. The backup covers absences and overflow. If the queue regularly exceeds that capacity, the manager shifts planned work or adds coverage. Review time comes out of existing commitments.

### How it is measured

- Start the clock when a human-authored PR is marked ready and review is requested. Count eight hours within the team’s published working schedule, excluding weekends and team holidays. Reassignment leaves the original deadline in place.
- Stop the clock at a human approval, a review with actionable feedback, or a concrete blocker explaining what the author needs to provide. Acknowledgments, emoji, and assignment changes leave the clock running. Track re-review turnaround separately so a quick first response cannot hide a stalled PR.
- Count each PR in the week its deadline falls. An unanswered PR is a miss when its deadline passes. Exclude bots and PRs withdrawn before the deadline, and show withdrawal counts so a rise is visible.
- Before launch, recalculate the prior four weeks using these rules. Show the reported 2.6-day median alongside the recalculated baseline if the timing definitions differ. Each week, two rotating engineers sample five credited responses and check whether the feedback is specific and the depth fits the change. Correct the score for any sampled response that fails.

### What engineers see

Use the existing team channel and PR queue. The queue shows the duty pair, outstanding requests, and time remaining. One reminder goes to the duty pair when a request has two working hours left. The weekly update shows the team’s on-time percentage and raw counts, median response time, oldest open request, re-review turnaround, and audit findings without naming individuals. The queue gives authors and reviewers the information they need to act. The weekly update shows whether the pilot is working.

### How it ends and what could go wrong

At week six, publish the result, grant the reward if earned, and retire the reward and pilot reporting. Keep the rota and queue reminders only if turnaround improved and the team can sustain the review time within planned capacity.

- **Rubber-stamping or token feedback.** Use the audit to catch shallow approvals and vague blockers. Review any missed defect raised during the pilot for signs that deadline pressure cut the review short.
- **Cherry-picking and clock resets.** The duty pair checks the oldest requests first and routes specialist work early. Review withdrawals and repeated draft-to-ready transitions for patterns. Returning a PR to draft leaves its original deadline in place.
- **Interruptions and uneven workload.** Ask once a week whether reviews displaced planned work or spilled into evenings. If they did, reduce other commitments or redistribute coverage that week. Pause the pilot if the team can only hit the target through overtime or shallower reviews.
