## One working day for a useful code review

Run a six-week team pilot. In a 20-minute paid session, ask engineers who regularly submit and review PRs where reviews stall and what makes a response useful. Fix the top blocker before launch. For example, if requests sit with people who are out or overloaded, assign a rotating backup and protect a short daily review block. Tell the team what they raised and what changed.

### What earns the reward

**A PR counts once when a human reviewer other than the author submits a useful review within one working day of the PR becoming ready.** An approval after checking the change, a request for changes, or a review that identifies a specific blocker and the next step all qualify. Acknowledgements, bot activity, and duplicate reviews earn nothing. A complex PR can get a useful response without being ready to merge.

Announce the reward at launch. If at least **85% of PRs receive a qualifying response in each of two weeks** that pass the quality check, everyone gets one paid half-day off, staggered for coverage. The engineering lead announces it at the next team meeting and names what the team did to make reviews faster. The qualifying weeks can be nonconsecutive. After an absence, an engineer simply picks up the next review in their queue.

### Measurement and visibility

Use the code host’s existing PR and submitted-review events. Start the clock when a PR is opened as ready or first leaves draft, even if nobody has assigned a reviewer. One working day means eight hours on the team’s agreed working calendar, excluding weekends and team holidays. Group PRs by the week their deadline falls so every PR in the weekly total has had its full window. Report the percentage that qualified alongside the numerator and denominator. Use the same rule to calculate a four-week baseline before launch. The current 2.6-day median describes the delay, but the percentage within one working day is the measure the pilot will use.

Engineers see their own outstanding requests, oldest first, and get private confirmation when their submitted review counts. Their personal target is to answer the requests they take on by their deadlines, with assignments sized to their availability. Post the weekly team total in the channel they already use. Managers get the aggregate only. There is no named leaderboard, individual reporting to managers, or use in performance reviews.

### Quality and failure modes

Before launch, test the rule on a duplicated review, a PR moved back to draft or reopened, and a review given in chat. A duplicate still counts once, moving a PR back to draft or reopening it keeps its original clock, and a chat-only review stays in the denominator without qualifying. Closing a ready PR also leaves it in the denominator, so closing slow PRs cannot improve the score.

Each week, the peer review captain samples five credited reviews, or all of them if fewer than five qualify. Check whether the review shows a reasonable check for the size and risk of the change or gives a concrete blocker and next step. Two inadequate reviews in the sample, or one clear bypass of a required check, pauses reward credit for that week. The engineering lead decides when the fix is in place and credit can resume. The captain corrects disputed counts from the PR record. Use the rotation to rebalance load if the same people keep picking up the queue. Automate the weekly count and budget 45 minutes for the sample, corrections, and team update.

### How it ends

By week four, make the oldest-first review queue the default view and route unattended requests to the backup. Reward accrual ends after week six. Recheck the on-time percentage in weeks three and four after rewards end. **Keep** the workflow at 85% or above, **change** it if it improves on baseline but misses 85%, and **drop** the pilot-specific workflow if it delivers no improvement over baseline. The half-day is a one-time reward.
