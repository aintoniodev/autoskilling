## One working day for a first code review

Run a four-week trial for the 25-engineer team. In the launch message, name the reward: everyone gets two hours of paid time off if at least 85% of eligible PRs get a substantive first review within one working day during the final two weeks. The engineering lead announces the result at the team meeting, thanks the team for specific improvements, and makes sure everyone can take the time without leaving the review queue uncovered.

### Fix the queue before launch

Ask a few frequent authors and reviewers what causes waits, then walk through several slow PRs together during paid work time. Fix the biggest routing or capacity problem and show the team what changed. Make the ready-for-review queue the default in the team’s existing Git workflow, oldest due first. Give the rotating reviewer two protected review blocks per day and name a backup for absences and overloaded days. If the team still lacks capacity to cover the queue, resolve that before starting the reward trial.

### What counts

Count the first submitted human review on each eligible PR using the existing Git events. A qualifying review is an approval with a brief note on what was checked, a concrete change request, or a specific blocking question. An acknowledgement, a bot comment, or a self-review earns no credit. A good first review can request changes and still count even if the PR takes longer to merge.

Start the clock at the PR’s first ready-for-review event, including PRs opened as ready. One working day means eight hours on the team’s agreed coverage calendar, excluding nights, weekends, and team holidays. Reassigning a reviewer, pushing another commit, toggling draft status, or reopening the PR leaves the original clock intact. Exclude automated PRs and genuine withdrawals before the deadline. A ready PR handled only in chat or left unassigned still enters the denominator. Use the week in which the deadline falls so late and unanswered PRs stay visible.

Before launch, test the count against a duplicate review, a dismissed review, a draft toggle, a genuine withdrawal, and a review handled only in chat. Publish the rule in one sentence: “A ready PR counts once when another engineer submits a substantive review in Git within eight hours of team coverage.”

### What engineers see

Use the existing Git notification to give authors immediate feedback. Give reviewers a private view of their own due reviews and on-time completions. The personal goal is to answer each assigned review by its deadline or hand it to the backup early enough for them to answer. A missed case leaves the next one fully achievable.

Post the team’s weekly on-time count and denominator in its usual channel. Managers get that same aggregate view. Keep programme data out of performance reviews and use no named rankings.

### What can go wrong

- **Rubber stamps or rushed feedback.** A rotating engineer samples five credited reviews each week across authors and reviewers. Check whether the note or feedback shows enough engagement with the diff for the author to act or proceed. If two or more fail, the engineering lead pauses the reward decision, fixes the cause with the team, and resumes after a fresh sample passes. A dismissed review that was invalid loses its credit.
- **Hidden waits or cherry-picking.** Review withdrawals and draft toggles during the weekly check, and route work by deadline. When a PR needs specialist input, the on-duty reviewer gets that person involved while keeping ownership of the deadline.
- **Review duty crowds out delivery.** Protect the review blocks in sprint planning. When the queue exceeds what the on-duty reviewer can cover, the backup takes work that day and the lead adjusts planned capacity. Engineers report count errors privately to the rotation owner, who checks the Git record and corrects the team total.

### How it ends

Before launch, record the previous four weeks’ **share of eligible PRs receiving a substantive first review within one working day**. The current 2.6-day median gives context, but the on-time share is the decision metric. Where historical records are ambiguous, label the baseline as an estimate.

End the reward after week four and keep the default queue and review coverage in place. Recalculate the same share over the four reward-free weeks. Keep the workflow at 85% or better. If it improves on baseline but falls short of 85%, change the biggest remaining bottleneck. If it fails to beat baseline, drop the incentive programme and revisit the queue and staffing. The weekly count, five-review sample, and corrections should take the rotation owner under an hour.
