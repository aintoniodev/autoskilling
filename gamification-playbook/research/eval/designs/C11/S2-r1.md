## One working day for a first code review

Run a four-week team pilot. Before launch, ask a few engineers who regularly author and review PRs where reviews get stuck. Fix the main bottleneck first. Start with a shared review queue and a protected 30-minute review block each working day. Assign a rotating reviewer and a backup so every ready PR has someone responsible for answering.

**What earns credit.** A PR earns one credit when a teammate submits a substantive first review within eight working hours of the PR becoming ready for review. A substantive review is an approval after checking the change, a request for changes, or feedback specific enough for the author to act on. A quick acknowledgment or emoji earns no credit. The review can arrive through the usual workflow.

**How it’s measured.** Use existing PR and review timestamps and the team’s published working hours and holiday calendar. Divide the number of timely first reviews by the number of eligible PRs whose deadline has passed. Count each PR once. Reassignment, reopening, and toggling draft status after the clock starts leave the original deadline in place. Exclude bot-authored PRs and genuine withdrawals before the deadline. An unanswered PR counts as late once its deadline passes. Recalculate the previous four weeks using this rule to establish the baseline. Today’s 2.6-day median gives context, while the pilot uses the percentage answered on time.

**What people see and earn.** Use the existing PR notification to confirm when a review counted. Engineers can privately see their own timely reviews against the cases they owned, with a personal target of four in five. With fewer than five cases, show the count and carry it forward across the pilot. Post one team percentage each week in the engineering channel. Managers receive the team total, and individual counts stay out of performance reviews. Announce the reward at launch. If the team reaches **85% on time across the pilot**, it earns a team-funded lunch during working hours, with an equivalent meal allowance for remote teammates. Everyone shares in it, including people who were on leave.

**What could go wrong.** People may rubber-stamp changes, cherry-pick easy PRs, or delay marking work ready. Keep the queue oldest-first and have the backup route specialist reviews early. Each week, a rotating peer samples ten credited PRs, or all of them if fewer than ten qualified, and checks whether the review engaged with the change. Two shallow or incorrectly credited reviews in a sample trigger a reward pause. The engineering lead decides when counting can resume after the cause is addressed. The pilot owner handles private disputes and corrects wrong counts. Test duplicates, reopened PRs, holidays, and reviews submitted without the queue before launch. Keep weekly administration under an hour.

**How it ends.** Before week four ends, make the shared queue the default review view and put reviewer coverage on the team calendar. Award the lunch once the final PR deadlines and quality check have cleared. Measure the same on-time rate over the next four weeks. At **85% or higher**, keep the workflow. Below 85% but at least **10 percentage points above baseline**, fix the remaining bottleneck. With a smaller gain, retire the programme. The prize ends after the pilot in all three cases.
