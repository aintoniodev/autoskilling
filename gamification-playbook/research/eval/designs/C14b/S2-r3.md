## One working day for a first code review

**Fix the review queue first.** In week 1, use part of an existing retro to ask authors and reviewers what makes reviews wait. Give each product area a rotating primary reviewer and backup, protect 20 minutes per working day for reviews, and put the oldest ready PRs first in the team’s existing review queue. Give engineers paid time to try the workflow. In week 2, publish the fixes alongside the friction people reported and show everyone how the count works.

**Reward a completed first review.** A PR earns one credit when a human submits its first meaningful review within eight working hours of becoming ready. An approval after checking the change, a request for changes with actionable feedback, or a submitted review with a concrete blocking question qualifies. An acknowledgement or emoji does not. At launch, announce the reward: if at least 80% of eligible PRs in a review rotation get an on-time first review over its four-week window, everyone in that rotation earns a half-day of paid time off, scheduled around coverage. The engineering lead announces the result in person at the existing team meeting.

**Measure from the PR history.** Use timestamps the code host already records. Start the clock when a human-authored PR first becomes ready for review, or when it opens if it opens ready. Count eight hours on the team’s published working-hours calendar, skipping weekends and team holidays. Group PRs by the week their deadline falls in so overdue PRs with no review count as misses. Reassignment, another review, or a return to draft does not restart the clock. Exclude bot PRs and PRs withdrawn before the deadline without merging. A PR merged without a qualifying review is a miss. Before launch, verify the rule against a duplicate review, a dismissed approval, a Friday handoff, and a PR handled through chat. A dismissed review loses its credit, and a chat response counts only if the reviewer records the outcome on the PR before the deadline.

**Give people feedback where they already work.** Confirm privately on submission whether the review counted and why. Give each reviewer a private goal based on their own volume, such as “answer your next assigned PR within a working day” for someone with one assignment, or “answer four of your next five” for someone with a steady queue. Progress carries across weeks, so leave costs no streak. Show only rotation totals in the existing weekly team update. Managers get the same totals, with no individual drill-down, and the counts stay out of performance reviews.

**Catch shortcuts and bad counts.** In the existing weekly engineering QA review, sample four credited PRs across the rotations, including quick approvals. Check whether the review engaged with the change and gave the author a clear decision or next step. If two of the last eight sampled credits fail, the engineering lead pauses reward accrual and addresses the cause with the team. The review rotation owner corrects disputed counts from the PR history before payout. Use the backup when someone is out or the queue spikes. Keep counting from the ready timestamp so leaving a PR unassigned cannot improve the score. Budget 45 minutes a week for the rotation owner to check the totals, prepare the sample, and resolve corrections.

**Run it once and check what sticks.** Give everyone the queue fixes and private feedback in weeks 1–2. Start the four-week reward window in week 3 for one existing review rotation and week 5 for the other. Use the two-week overlap to see whether the reward adds anything beyond the shared workflow changes. When each reward window closes, keep the oldest-first queue, protected review time, and backup coverage as defaults. Four weeks after the last reward ends, use one number to decide: the share of eligible PRs reviewed within one working day. At **80% or higher**, keep the workflow. At **65–79%**, adjust queue ownership or protected time and recheck. **Below 65%**, retire the incentive and use the missed PRs to identify the biggest workflow bottleneck.
