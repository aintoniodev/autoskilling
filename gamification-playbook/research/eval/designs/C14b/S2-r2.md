## One-working-day review pilot

**Reward a useful first review on a real pull request.** A review counts when a human reviewer submits an approval, a request for changes, or specific feedback the author can act on. The author should know whether they can proceed or what needs attention. An approval counts without a written comment when the reviewer has checked the change. An acknowledgment such as “I’ll look tomorrow” leaves the clock running. The target is the first useful response. The author and reviewer can take the time they need to resolve the issues it raises.

**Fix the queue first.** In week one, ask a few engineers who regularly author and review PRs where requests get stuck. Give everyone 30 minutes of protected time to try the revised workflow. Use the existing code-owner rules to route ready PRs to a rotating primary and backup, give the rotation a protected review block each working day, and put the oldest waiting PRs first in the queue. Tell the team which problems they raised and what changed. Start the reward phase once the rotation has reliable cover.

**Measure one event per PR.** Use existing repository timestamps. The clock starts when a PR first becomes ready for review and runs for eight hours on the published team working calendar, excluding weekends and team holidays. Credit the first qualifying submitted review from someone other than the author. Count each PR once. A second review, a new commit, or a draft-and-reopen cycle earns no extra credit and leaves the original deadline in place. Exclude PRs genuinely withdrawn before the deadline. Keep unreviewed PRs and withdrawals after the deadline in the denominator as misses. Before launch, test a duplicate review, a reopened PR, a withdrawal, an existing review submitted through the usual workflow, and a Friday handoff. Recalculate the previous four weeks with this definition so the pilot has a comparable baseline alongside the reported 2.6-day median.

**Show people their queue and the team its progress.** Use the repository’s existing review notifications for immediate confirmation that a review was submitted. Reviewers use their own queue to see what is due and set a private goal sized to their workload, such as answering the next three assigned PRs on time. After a missed day, the next PR is a fresh chance. Post the team’s on-time percentage and PR count in the existing engineering channel once a week. Managers receive team totals only, with no per-person drill-down or use in performance reviews.

**Announce a reward engineers have helped choose.** At launch, offer each engineer in a participating group one protected paid hour to use as they choose if the group answers at least 70% of eligible PRs on time over its two reward weeks and clears the quality check. Confirm that the team values the hour during week one. The engineering lead recognizes the group at the existing team meeting and puts the time on the calendar, with staggered cover.

**Run for six weeks and check what sticks.** Give everyone the queue fixes and feedback during weeks 1–2. Use two existing review groups with similar PR volume, if available, and randomly choose which starts rewards in weeks 3–4. The other gets its reward window in weeks 5–6. Compare the groups during weeks 3–4 as a directional check on what the reward adds. If ownership overlaps too heavily, run one team-wide reward window in weeks 3–4 and treat the comparison as before-and-after. Keep the queue, rotation and protected review block as defaults after rewards end. Four weeks later, use the on-time rate over those four weeks to decide whether to keep the workflow as configured (70% or higher), adjust it (55–69%), or drop the pilot’s extra routines and revisit the bottleneck (below 55%). Retire the reward at the end of its window.

**Watch for shallow reviews and overloaded reviewers.** In the existing engineering meeting, sample five credited reviews each week across easy and complex changes, or all credited reviews if fewer than five. Check whether each response gave the author a clear next step and showed appropriate care for the change. If two fail, the engineering lead pauses the payout, corrects any counts, and reviews the issue with the rotation. Engineers can flag a wrong count privately to the lead. If people start prioritizing easy PRs or the review block crowds out planned work, route older requests to the backup and adjust capacity before the next reward window. The lead owns the weekly tally and sample, with a 45-minute budget.
