## One-working-day code review pilot

Give each ready PR a useful first review within one working day. The current median is 2.6 days. Ask engineers who regularly author and review PRs what slows them down, then give them paid work time to try the fixes. Start with a clear reviewer and backup, a protected review window, and one queue in the tool they already use. Tell the team which friction they reported and what changed.

### What earns the reward

**A ready PR counts once when someone other than its author submits a useful review within one working day.** An approval after checking the change, a specific change request, or a question the author needs to answer qualifies. An acknowledgment, emoji, or “I’ll look later” does not. A review can happen live if the reviewer records the decision or next step in the PR before the deadline.

Announce the reward at launch. If at least **80% of eligible PRs** receive an on-time review during a group’s four-week reward window, everyone in that group earns one paid hour off. The engineering lead thanks the group in person, names what helped the queue move, and schedules coverage so everyone can take the hour. There are no individual prizes to compete for.

### How it’s measured

Use existing PR timestamps and submitted review events. Start the clock when a PR first becomes ready for review, or when it opens if it opens ready. Count eight hours on the team’s published working calendar, skipping nights, weekends, and team holidays. A reviewer’s absence triggers backup coverage. It does not reset the clock.

Divide the number of eligible PRs with an on-time first review by the number of eligible PRs whose deadline fell in the measurement window. Before launch, run the rule on recent PRs to establish the baseline and test the awkward cases. A second reviewer or additional review round earns no extra credit. Moving a PR back to draft never earns extra time. A withdrawn approval loses credit unless another qualifying review was submitted by the original deadline. A PR merged without a qualifying review counts as a miss. A closed PR can be excluded only if the pilot lead verifies it was genuinely abandoned or superseded.

Each week, a rotating engineer checks a random sample of ten credited reviews, pooling across weeks when volume is low. **Two unearned credits in ten pause reward awards.** The engineering lead decides when to resume. The pilot lead corrects the counts and tells affected engineers privately. A terse approval can be valid, so the audit checks whether the review was appropriate for the change, without requiring boilerplate.

### What people see

After a qualifying review, the reviewer gets a private confirmation on the existing review surface, such as “PR #482 got its first review in 5 working hours.” Their private view shows their own assigned first-review opportunities and which they handled on time. The personal goal is to answer or arrange backup for the next assigned review within a working day. A missed week carries no penalty.

Show the team’s rate and denominator where they already check work, with group totals during each group’s reward window. Managers get aggregate results with no individual drill-down. Individual counts stay out of performance reviews. There is no named leaderboard.

### Timing and decision

Pull a four-week baseline, then give everyone two weeks with the queue, protected review time, and feedback. Start the four-week reward window with one existing review group and start the other two weeks later. Use the overlap to see whether the reward appears to add anything beyond the workflow fixes. With 25 engineers, treat that comparison as directional.

Before the first reward window ends, make the ready-for-review queue and backup routing the default for everyone. End each group’s reward on schedule. Four weeks after the last reward ends, use the team’s **on-time first-review rate** to decide what to do with the workflow: **80% or higher**, keep it as the default. **65–79%**, fix the biggest remaining source of delay. **Below 65%**, end the pilot and revisit reviewer capacity and ownership.

### What could go wrong

Engineers may rush approvals, pick easy PRs, or delay marking hard PRs ready. The quality audit catches shallow reviews, assigning the oldest due PR first limits cherry-picking, and a weekly look at aging drafts in the existing queue surfaces work held outside the clock. Heavy review load may also squeeze out product work. If that happens, shift coverage or reduce competing work so engineers can review during paid hours.
