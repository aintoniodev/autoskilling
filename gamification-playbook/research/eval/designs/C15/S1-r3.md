## Pre-job safety checklist pilot

**Start with the technicians.** Give technicians from different shifts, routes, and coverage areas paid time to walk through real jobs with the union steward, dispatch, and safety lead. Fix the problems they find before rewards begin. Put the checklist first on the job screen, prefill job details, and make it usable offline. Tell crews what changed because of their feedback. Agree with the union on the reward, eligibility, data access, and how technicians can challenge a count.

**Reward one completed safety check per job.** Publish this rule at launch: “A job counts once when you complete its digital safety checklist before work starts, or before dispatch records that the job is on hold for a hazard you identified.” A hazard hold earns the same credit as a job cleared to start. If a held job resumes, require a fresh check before work starts and keep the reward at one credit for the job.

Offer every technician in a crew two hours of paid release time, scheduled during their normal shift, if the crew reaches **75% of eligible jobs** over its six-week reward window and passes the quality check. Confirm with technicians and the union that the reward is worth earning and can be scheduled. Name it in the launch message and have the crew lead recognize what the team did when announcing the award.

**Measure it from existing job records.** Divide qualifying jobs by all jobs that started or were placed on a confirmed safety hold. Use the app’s checklist completion record and the existing dispatch start or hold record. Validate before launch that those records establish the order reliably, including offline jobs. Count cancelled jobs only if they had already started or been put on a confirmed safety hold. Keep jobs completed with the paper fallback in the denominator and show app outages alongside the result. Paper stays available whenever the app fails so technicians can follow the safety procedure and report hazards.

Before launch, run sample jobs through the rule. A duplicate submission earns one credit. A checklist voided before work starts loses credit unless replaced in time. A checklist submitted after work starts earns none. An offline checklist synced later earns credit if the existing records verify it was completed in time. The safety lead decides disputes with the union steward, and the dispatch system owner corrects the count.

**Show progress where people already look.** After each submission, show the technician “counted,” “pending sync,” or the reason it did not count. Each technician sees their own qualifying jobs and a goal sized to their actual workload, such as four of their next five jobs. Show crew progress in the existing safety huddle or crew channel. Managers see crew totals only, with small crews pooled where totals could identify individuals. Keep programme data out of performance reviews. A missed week carries no penalty. The next eligible job is another chance to contribute.

**Run it in two waves and end on schedule.** Give all crews the fixes, paid practice, and feedback first. Measure two weeks of adoption with those changes in place. Assign comparable crews to two waves, starting the second wave three weeks after the first. Each crew gets six reward weeks. Compare the waves during those first three weeks to estimate what the reward adds. When each crew’s reward window closes, keep the checklist first in the app and keep the submission feedback. Use the same completion rate to decide what stays four weeks after rewards end. At **75% or higher**, keep the workflow and feedback. At **60–74%**, fix the most common remaining barrier and recheck. Below **60%**, retire the incentive and revisit the workflow with technicians.

**Watch for rushed checks, false holds, and peer pressure.** Each week, the safety lead reviews 20 randomly selected credited jobs across active crews against existing records, including a sample of hazard holds where available. Pause reward awards if more than 10% have a material defect, such as a missing required hazard assessment, or if a confirmed case shows work proceeded despite an unresolved serious hazard. The safety lead and union steward review the cause and decide when awards can resume. Handle hazards immediately through the existing safety process, protect the technician’s right to stop work, and keep individual results out of crew discussions.
