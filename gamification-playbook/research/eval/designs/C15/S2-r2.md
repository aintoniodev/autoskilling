# One-working-day code review pilot

**Start by fixing the queue.** In weeks 1–2, ask engineers who regularly wait for reviews and engineers who field most requests where reviews get stuck. Give them paid time to test the fixes. Use the existing GitHub ownership rules to route requests to a rotating reviewer and backup. Protect a daily review block during working hours and remove an equivalent amount of planned delivery work from the rotation. Put the oldest review requests first in the team’s existing queue. Tell the team which changes came from their feedback. Give everyone the queue improvements and private feedback before rewards begin.

**Reward a useful first review.** Publish the rule at launch: “A ready PR earns one credit when a requested human reviewer submits a substantive review within one working day.” An approval that has actually checked the change, a request for changes, or a submitted review with an actionable question qualifies. An assignment, emoji, bot response, or “looking now” does not. Authors can act on the review even if the PR takes longer to merge.

Use existing GitHub events. The clock starts when the PR is both ready and has a human review request. One working day means eight hours on the team’s published working calendar, excluding weekends and holidays. Reviews outside working hours are optional. Reassignment, new commits, and returning to draft do not restart the clock. Count each PR once. For example, two qualifying reviews earn one credit, a rubber-stamp approval earns none, and a PR withdrawn before its deadline leaves the denominator. A PR withdrawn after missing its deadline stays in the denominator. The review rotation lead corrects disputed counts privately and the pilot owner resolves disagreements.

**Show progress where engineers already work.** After a qualifying review, privately confirm which PR counted and show the reviewer their own progress. Use a personal target of four on-time reviews out of every five assigned during their rotation, prorated to actual volume. Show “2 of 3 answered on time” for low volumes and carry progress across weeks. Missing a review or taking leave does not reset progress or affect eligibility. In the existing team channel, post the weekly team percentage and the underlying counts. Managers see aggregate results only. Personal counts never enter performance reviews, and there is no named leaderboard.

**Name the reward up front.** Launch with “If we answer at least 80% of eligible PRs within one working day during our four reward weeks, everyone in the group earns a paid half-day off.” Confirm the reward choice with engineers before launch. The engineering lead announces the earned reward at the next team meeting and arranges staggered time off with backup coverage. There is no individual quota for the reward.

**Check quality before paying out.** Each week, a rotating senior engineer outside the sampled reviews checks five randomly selected credited PRs for evidence that the reviewer engaged with the change. A brief approval can be adequate for a straightforward diff. Two inadequate reviews in the latest ten sampled trigger a pause on awarding the reward. The pilot owner decides when to resume after the rotation lead fixes the cause and corrects affected counts. Watch for difficult PRs being left to the backup, requests being delayed to make the clock look better, or reviewers working after hours. Discuss those patterns at the team retro and adjust routing or capacity.

**End the rewards and check what sticks.** Start rewards with one existing review group in week 3 and the other in week 5, using stable ownership groups so people rarely review across them. Each group gets four reward weeks. Keep the rotation, protected review time, and oldest-first queue as defaults before its reward period ends. If the team shares one queue and cannot form sensible groups, run a single four-week reward window and compare it with the fixes-only period. Treat that comparison as directional.

Use one decision metric throughout: the percentage of eligible PRs receiving a substantive first review within one working day. Reconstruct it from recent GitHub history where possible, since the current 2.6-day median does not tell you that percentage. Check again four weeks after rewards end. At **80% or higher**, keep the workflow and retire the reward. At **70–79%**, adjust capacity or routing and recheck after four weeks. Below **70%**, drop the incentive programme and revisit the bottleneck with engineers. If the fixes-only period already reaches 80%, skip the reward and check whether the workflow holds.
