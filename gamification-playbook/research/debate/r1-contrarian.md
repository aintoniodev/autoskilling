# Round 1 — Contrarian

**Keep the safeguards. Stop making the motivating part an afterthought.** Findings is right that a game cannot fix a bad workflow. But a good workflow can sit unused if the first try feels risky, little happens when it succeeds, and nobody sees a peer make it work. A short-lived lift can be valuable if it buys enough verified practice to learn the tool's value. That is a hypothesis about the route to persistence, not evidence of persistence.

## Ranking and discriminating checks

Ranked by what I would emphasize in a short skill, not by whether a safeguard is dispensable. The last column is a **blind design check** inside the existing with/without-skill eval, not a proposed employee experiment we can run now. Judge whether the plan makes the check possible; it cannot establish how employees would respond.

| Rank | Hypothesis and why | Smallest break | What the judge should be able to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **H1 — Quality-qualified work.** Strong incentives are defensible only if winning advances the real outcome. | A manual first send followed by an AI replacement wins. | Trace those events and an AI first send recalled for error through target, credit, reversal and quality separately. |
| 2 | **H7 — Conditional peer competition.** A visible, winnable race can make progress socially meaningful in a way private bars cannot. | Halfway through, a median participant can no longer catch anyone. | In a suitable scenario, name the audience, reset or reachable division, opportunity adjustment, opt-out and a rank-free comparator. | 3 | **H3 — Workflow cue and value.** Rewards buy worthwhile practice only when the supported route can win on its own. | QA erases the advertised time saving. | Reconstruct one ordinary attempt and one exception, including queue cue, rework, fallback and who fixes the blocker. |
| 4 | **H2 — Attainable goals.** A challenging visible milestone can recruit effort before anyone will care about rank. | One easy case puts someone at 100%. | Score books of 0, 1 and 20 eligible cases with uneven complexity; verify each person sees a defensible next step. |
| 5 | **H6 — Recognition and rewards.** An earned symbol can advertise proficiency; a modest prize can cover a real cost of trying. | A badge certifies a shortcut anyone can farm. | Identify the behavior a recipient would repeat, attempt the shortcut, and distinguish useful feedback from badge or prize. If a prize is included, look for a cost ceiling and a reason recognition alone may be insufficient. |
| 6 | **H9 — Fast payoff, honest settlement.** Acknowledgement that arrives after the teachable moment loses value; payment need not arrive with it. | The celebration says “earned” before the event can be verified. | Walk through the same valid and reversed event in the immediate view and settlement ledger. Make clear what the manager never sees. |

## Evidence the skill should not flatten

- **Public comparison and team tournaments.** [Delfgaauw et al.](https://docs.iza.org/dp4395.pdf) randomized short retail-store contests with weekly within-pool rankings; the sales lift was concentrated in a subgroup and similar with and without money. This supports visible *team* standings as a candidate, not an all-company named rep board or demographic screening. [Bradler et al.](https://docs.iza.org/dp8311.pdf) found public thank-you cards to the top three of eight lifted subsequent data-entry performance by 7.3%, mainly through nonrecipients; the remaining task lasted about an hour. A consented top-only board or a public demonstration badge may give observers a reachable standard without naming the bottom. Neither isolates live named individual rank. For badge-like stakes, [Kosfeld and Neckermann](https://doi.org/10.1257/mic.3.3.86) (abstract) found announcing a symbolic best-performance card boosted output about 12% on a short paid database project; that tests a status award, not collecting routine badges.
- **Tangible prizes, immediate rewards and loss.** A team sales bonus raised sales and customer counts by 3% in [Friebel et al.](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20160788) (abstract), without requiring rank. A prize can be useful when the expected effort is real, not as a default substitute for useful work. In [Hossain and List](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w15623/w15623.pdf), provisional bonus loss framing raised team output roughly 1% relative to equivalent gain framing; [Levitt et al.](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w18165/w18165.pdf) found no loss advantage in student testing, but delayed rewards lost their motivating effect there. Test keeping a game-only shield rather than earning it, never taking wages or vested rewards. [Duolingo's own report](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit/) says early streak animations improved seven-day retention by 1.7% as reported and adding a second freeze increased relative daily activity by 0.38%. Vendor evidence, not workplace transfer. It is a concrete reason to test a brief, optional celebration on top of identical acknowledgement, not to conflate the two.
- **Manager visibility and missing mechanisms.** [Sandvik et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w29148) found assigning mentors in the non-opt-in arm raised new sales call-center workers' daily revenue 17% in their first two months. This tests support, not dashboard access; it suggests giving someone with time and authority a narrow signal they can act on, rather than waiting for the people who need help to ask. For H12, [Grant](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.93.1.108) (abstract) reports field effects of task significance on fundraising performance and lifeguard helping. For H13, [Hallsworth et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(16)00215-4) (abstract) found 3.3% fewer antibiotics dispensed with a trusted messenger's comparative feedback package; it bundled normative feedback with other material, so the norm alone is unproven.

**Could the cautious skill win and still produce timid designs? Yes.** The rubric permits a ten on incentive choice for timely, specific feedback; it does not explicitly ask whether employees will notice the offer, see a reason to try, or know what to do next when behind. Don't award points for toys or change the primary score. Before judging, freeze a separate blind diagnostic: quote the actual launch message or first-use screen, the first credible payoff and the next move for someone behind. If these can't be found where the brief warrants an incentive, flag low salience even on a high-scoring plan. Conversely, a private workflow cue can pass if it does that job. A higher design score cannot resolve actual adoption, net lift or persistence.
