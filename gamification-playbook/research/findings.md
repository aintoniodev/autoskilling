# Workplace incentive programmes: evidence and design hypotheses

The best-supported design is **a useful workflow with a trustworthy outcome measure, attainable goals, feedback workers can use, and a test of what survives after rewards end**. A scoreboard can change behaviour without improving the work. No source here estimates the incremental effect of a game on AI renewal quoting; the case's 40% target is a business objective, not an evidence-based forecast.

## Scope and method

This is a targeted evidence scan, not a systematic review. Discovery used discipline-specific web searches in OpenAlex and Crossref plus general web queries; claims below were checked against opened papers, author manuscripts, official records or abstract records. **[Abstract only]** means findings were verified only from a source abstract, not its methods or tables. Working papers and postmortems are labelled. Grades describe the evidence design behind the indicated claim, not expected effect in this workplace: **A** meta-analysis or multiple field RCTs; **B** one field RCT or strong quasi-/natural experiment; **C** lab or observational; **D** theory, expert opinion, postmortem or untested design inference. A grade never promotes an adjacent claim (e.g., gamification bundles → confetti alone) or converts associations into causal estimates. Several reviews reuse studies; they do not count as independent replications.

## What bounds durable behaviour?

- **Opportunity and capability.** If only F/N opportunities have a safe, available workflow, even universal use when feasible gives at most F/N overall adoption today. Audit access, training, exception types, tool errors and ownership first. Keep the all-due rate visible alongside a predeclared feasible-opportunity rate and blocker mix. Retrospective exclusion can make a ceiling disappear on paper while real coverage is unchanged.
- **Value at the point of work.** A rep adopts when perceived time/quality benefits and an easy cue outweigh learning, review, rework, reliability and risk costs. Rewards only shift that balance while present unless users learn lasting value, reduce skill costs, build repeatable cues or the organisation changes the workflow. If the manual route remains safer or quicker after ramp-up, more points won't fix that. Distinguish reasoned tool choice from automatic cue-driven behaviour: persistence can be valuable without proving automaticity.
- **Measurement and displacement.** Crediting one observed proxy raises the return to whichever action produces it most cheaply, whether that is good work, easy-case selection or a false event. Verify independent customer/quality outcomes, omitted work, and untreated responsibilities. A technically exact computation can faithfully measure the wrong construct.
- **Attention and pressure.** New announcements, managerial attention, and public comparison can change short-run priorities. A worker who is behind needs a credible next action; an unattainable rank or a single lost streak can remove that reason to try. Attention is finite and displaced from other work. Novelty decay is a candidate explanation for falling effects, not something one before/after trend identifies.
- **Identification and economics.** A 40-person workplace has correlated outcomes and varying opportunity volumes; 1,000 transactions from those people are not 1,000 independent assignments. Quantify incremental net value and uncertainty, including programme administration and rework. Use a comparison, baseline and follow-up; judge-design scores are an eval for the skill, not evidence of real-world adoption.

## Hypotheses for the skill

### H1. Reward quality-qualified work, not traces of activity — B for quantity response; D for specific anti-gaming design
**Case mapping:** supports 1 and 12; modifies 8. **Mechanism:** pay attention to the customer's outcome, not what is easiest to count. Work backward from the cheapest way to earn a credit and from work that could be displaced.

**Evidence.** Steven Kerr (1975), “On the Folly of Rewarding A, While Hoping for B,” *Academy of Management Journal*, 18(4), 769–783 ([original abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.2307/255378?select=biblio,abstract_inverted_index), **abstract only**; [1995 reprint](https://www.ou.edu/russell/UGcomp/Kerr.pdf)), illustrates the conflict between rewarded behaviour and desired behaviour; it is a management diagnosis, not a trial. Edward P. Lazear (2000), “Performance Pay and Productivity,” *American Economic Review*, 90(5), 1346–1361 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.90.5.1346), **abstract only**), estimates 44% more output per worker after an auto-glass company switched to piece rates; this includes selection and cannot forecast comparable quality or adoption gains. Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (2016), [“OCC Assesses Penalty Against Wells Fargo, Orders Restitution for Unsafe or Unsound Sales Practices”](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2016/nr-occ-2016-106.html), found unauthorised accounts and fund transfers and weak controls; the OCC assessed a $35 million penalty. This is a failure case, not a typical marginal effect.

**Failure/boundary.** “Sent” is better than “drafted” but not “correct,” “on time,” or necessarily “first.” If the target is first quote from the tool and credit is for the current quote in play, replacing a manual first quote with an AI quote can earn credit without moving the target. Conversely, an AI first send recalled for error can move a raw target but fail the quality gate. A hard on-time gate can prompt premature sending or hiding late but legitimate work. **Expected value:** fewer ways to win while harming customers; defensible linkage from points to outcomes. **Reviewer check:** write the event and denominator contract; table-test manual→AI, AI→manual, duplicate sends, adverse reversals and due-date edits; track late/suppressed sends, independently sampled material errors and recalls/replacements per send and per due opportunity. Name a pause threshold, minimum volume, owner and remedy; don't wait until payout to stop harm.

### H2. Give attainable, opportunity-adjusted goals and usable feedback — A for goals and feedback; D for any exact target/bracket formula
**Case mapping:** modifies 3 and 11; supports showing clear progress. **Mechanism:** a specific credible next step directs effort; feedback enables correction. Difficulty should reflect feasible opportunities, capability, and starting level rather than a raw count or arbitrary peer rank.

**Evidence.** Edwin A. Locke and Gary P. Latham (2002), “Building a Practically Useful Theory of Goal Setting and Task Motivation: A 35-Year Odyssey,” *American Psychologist*, 57(9), 705–717 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066x.57.9.705), **abstract only**), synthesise goal-setting results and moderators; the review does not validate any renewal target. Tracy Epton, Sinéad Currie and Christopher J. Armitage (2017), “Unique Effects of Setting Goals on Behavior Change: Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis,” *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, 85(12), 1182–1198 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000260), **abstract only**), estimate a unique goal-setting effect of d = 0.34 (95% CI 0.28–0.41) across 141 papers/16,523 people; moderator results favoured difficult, publicly set and group goals, not exposure of individual scores. Ben Harkin, Thomas L. Webb, Betty P. I. Chang, Andrew Prestwich, Mark Conner, Ian Kellar, Yael Benn and Paschal Sheeran (2016), “Does Monitoring Goal Progress Promote Goal Attainment? A Meta-Analysis of the Experimental Evidence,” *Psychological Bulletin*, 142(2), 198–229 ([accepted manuscript](https://cdr.lib.unc.edu/downloads/mc87px074)), report goal attainment d = 0.40 (95% CI 0.32–0.48) across 138 randomised comparisons/studies in their synthesis. Effects were heterogeneous; public reporting was a moderator, not a test of mandatory named rank or employer surveillance.

**Failure/boundary.** One success in one opportunity yields 100%; forty routine deals and forty complex deals are not equivalent; a cap can make extra work irrelevant. For a novel, complex task, a capability milestone (e.g., practise a supported type with feedback) can be safer than immediate quota pressure. A team finish line is shared information, not evidence that a team prize works. **Expected value:** fairer attainable progress and a better decision about where to provide support. **Reviewer check:** simulate low-, zero- and high-opportunity books, leave, transfers, blockers and mixed complexity; show n/N and a personal next action; calculate group rate from summed counts, not unweighted rep percentages; suppress identifiable small cells and check whether the benchmark is attainable without unsafe behaviour.

### H3. Put the cue and value in the workflow; address blockers before adding pressure — B for cue flexibility; C for automaticity and tool transfer
**Case mapping:** new; modifies 4 and 7. **Mechanism:** connect the action to a work event already encountered; reduce the cost of getting through it. Use “when an eligible renewal enters the first-quote queue, start from the tool and verify the result,” not “remember to feed the game every week.” Exceptions should feed product improvements, with a safe fallback.

**Evidence.** Wendy Wood and David T. Neal (2007), “A New Look at Habits and the Habit–Goal Interface,” *Psychological Review*, 114(4), 843–863 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.114.4.843), **abstract only**), describe repeated context–response pairings as a route to cue-triggered behaviour. Phillippa Lally, Cornelia H. M. van Jaarsveld, Henry W. W. Potts and Jane Wardle (2010), “How Are Habits Formed: Modelling Habit Formation in the Real World,” *European Journal of Social Psychology*, 40(6), 998–1009 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674), **abstract only**), found modelled times to 95% of automaticity ranging 18–254 days, and no material effect from one missed opportunity. This was a small self-report study of daily actions, with good model fit for only 39 participants, not an eight-week prescription. John Beshears, Hae Nim Lee, Katherine L. Milkman, Robert Mislavsky and Jessica Wisdom (2021), “Creating Exercise Habits Using Incentives: The Trade-off Between Flexibility and Routinization,” *Management Science*, 67(7), 4139–4171 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/W3093172502), **abstract only**), found flexible daily incentives produced more gym visits in and after the programme than requiring a specified two-hour window.

**Failure/boundary.** A cue cannot overcome missing access, an unsafe output, a case the technology cannot handle, or approval dependencies. Make the cue event-based, not a rigid schedule. If an employee works a renewal once a month, calendar duration is a poor measure of repetition. **Expected value:** more supported repetitions and a workflow worth keeping after the game. **Reviewer check:** observe several eligible and blocked examples end to end; compare tool and manual time including QA/rework, error rate and time-to-first-send; identify a named owner and response time for blocker remediation; retest changed friction before raising stakes.

### H4. Pre-plan withdrawal and measure persistence without prizes — A
**Case mapping:** modifies 7 and 11; recognises retirement as a design asset, not proof. **Mechanism:** temporary incentives can buy exploration or practice; expiry reveals whether the workflow has acquired enduring value. Keep helpful infrastructure while removing the active incentive and clearly defining which feedback remains.

**Evidence.** Gary Charness and Uri Gneezy (2009), “Incentives to Exercise,” *Econometrica*, 77(3), 909–931 ([abstract record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecm/emetrp/v77y2009i3p909-931.html), **abstract only**), report post-payment attendance increases driven by people who hadn't been regular gym users. Heather Royer, Mark Stehr and Justin Sydnor (2015), “Incentives, Commitments, and Habit Formation in Exercise: Evidence from a Field Experiment with Workers at a Fortune-500 Company,” *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 7(3), 51–84 ([journal abstract](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.20130327); [author working paper](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w18580/w18580.pdf)), find small carryover from four weeks of incentives alone but sizeable longer-term effects in the arm offered an optional self-funded commitment contract afterwards. This is an effect of the **offer**, not proof that requiring employee deposits works. Dan Acland and Matthew R. Levy (2015), “Naiveté, Projection Bias, and Habit Formation in Gym Attendance,” *Management Science*, 61(1), 146–160 ([accepted paper](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/66147/1/Levy_Naivet%C3%A9%2C%20projection_2016.pdf)), report an immediate post-treatment lift of +0.188 visits/week in their overview; no significant difference remained after winter break. Mariana Carrera, Heather Royer, Mark Stehr and Justin Sydnor (2018), “Can Financial Incentives Help People Trying to Establish New Habits? Experimental Evidence with New Gym Members,” *Journal of Health Economics*, 58, 202–214 ([author working-paper abstract](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23567), **abstract only**), report moderate attendance effects in the first six weeks and no subsequent lift among 836 new members.

**Failure/boundary.** Neither meeting the target while rewards are live nor a six-week best streak demonstrates persistence. A payout, countdown or equivalent new challenge during “follow-up” contaminates the test. The strongest retention evidence above comes from exercise, not complex paid work. **Expected value:** avoid maintaining a costly game for an effect that evaporates; detect which features should stay. **Reviewer check:** record ordinary baseline, intervention, post-closure/payout follow-up and a calendar- or opportunity-based success criterion up front; measure quality-adjusted first-use on subsequent opportunities, by cohort as well as overall. Log outages, seasonality and case-mix changes rather than labelling every change “novelty.”

### H5. Design re-entry, not a cliff — B for testing return incentives; C for transplanting streak repair
**Case mapping:** modifies 4, 7 and 10. **Mechanism:** an intact streak can become its own goal; a break can remove that goal. Expiry, leave and outages should not turn a recoverable lapse into a reason to give up. A restart can use a genuine work milestone rather than an artificial daily requirement.

**Evidence.** Jackie Silverman and Alixandra Barasch (2023; online 2022), “On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions,” *Journal of Consumer Research*, 49(6), 1095–1117 ([accepted manuscript](https://udspace.udel.edu/bitstreams/7e3e3b64-8bf2-4527-ac93-94a160358165/download)), find across seven studies that highlighting an intact rather than broken streak increased subsequent behaviour; the gap was attenuated by repair and stronger when people blamed themselves for a break. Hengchen Dai, Katherine L. Milkman and Jason Riis (2014), “The Fresh Start Effect: Temporal Landmarks Motivate Aspirational Behavior,” *Management Science*, 60(10), 2563–2582 ([abstract record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v60y2014i10p2563-2582.html), **abstract only**), report spikes in diet searches, gym visits and commitments after salient time boundaries in three archival studies. This concerns restarting or initiating pursuit, not lasting adherence. Katherine L. Milkman et al. (2021), “Megastudies Improve the Impact of Applied Behavioural Science,” *Nature*, 600, 478–483 ([article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04128-4)), tested 54 four-week interventions among 61,293 gym members; 45% of interventions significantly raised weekly visits by 9–27% during treatment. The top arm offered a microreward for returning after a missed workout; only 8% of interventions showed significant measurable effects after four weeks. This is an arm ranking, not a guarantee that the winning mechanism beats all close variants, and a return bonus is not proof that streaks themselves work. Lukas Moldon, Markus Strohmaier and Johannes Wachs (2021), “How Gamification Affects Software Developers: Cautionary Evidence from a Natural Experiment on GitHub,” *ICSE*, 549–561 ([author manuscript](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.02371)), found fewer long streaks, less weekend activity and fewer single-contribution days after public streak counters disappeared. Their traces don't establish code quality.

**Failure/boundary.** Do not create a reason to miss intentionally for a richer reward, or penalise leave, a zero-opportunity period or a safe manual fallback. Forgiven does not mean completed. **Expected value:** return after absence with less pressure to game the calendar. **Reviewer check:** replay a missed week, outage, vacation, mixed success/blocker week, and return; display qualification separately from repair and actual completed work; compare next eligible-opportunity return against plain reminders, checking for induced misses and weekend pressure.

### H6. Use informative recognition before higher-stakes tangible rewards — A for moderation; B for examples of reward choice
**Case mapping:** modifies 8, 10 and 13. **Mechanism:** specific, credible feedback can reinforce competence and signal contribution. A salient contingent payment can instead signal control, displace existing standards, elicit quantity or change what the action means.

**Evidence.** Edward L. Deci, Richard Koestner and Richard M. Ryan (1999), “A Meta-Analytic Review of Experiments Examining the Effects of Extrinsic Rewards on Intrinsic Motivation,” *Psychological Bulletin*, 125(6), 627–668 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627), **abstract only**), report free-choice d = −0.40, −0.36 and −0.28 for engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent rewards; positive feedback d = +0.33. Judy Cameron and W. David Pierce (1994), “Reinforcement, Reward, and Intrinsic Motivation: A Meta-Analysis,” *Review of Educational Research*, 64(3), 363–423 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543064003363), **abstract only**), found no overall decrement and chiefly a negative effect for expected tangible rewards for just doing a task; Cameron, Katherine M. Banko and Pierce (2001), “Pervasive Negative Effects of Rewards on Intrinsic Motivation: The Myth Continues,” *The Behavior Analyst*, 24, 1–44 ([article record with abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2731358/), **abstract only**), distinguish low-interest tasks and expected tangible rewards loosely tied to performance on high-interest tasks. These reviews disagree on the scope of negative effects and how to group contingencies, not on whether contingencies matter; lab free-choice is not employee retention. Christopher P. Cerasoli, Jessica M. Nicklin and Michael T. Ford (2014), “Intrinsic Motivation and Extrinsic Incentives Jointly Predict Performance: A 40-Year Meta-Analysis,” *Psychological Bulletin*, 140(4), 980–1008 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035661), **abstract only**), find intrinsic motivation more predictive of quality, incentives more predictive of quantity, and both jointly predictive; these predictor differences are not causal proof of crowd-out. Nava Ashraf, Oriana Bandiera and B. Kelsey Jack (2014), “No Margin, No Mission? A Field Experiment on Incentives for Public Service Delivery,” *Journal of Public Economics*, 120, 1–17 ([authors' evaluation report](https://doi.org/10.23846/ow1007)), report public-service agents assigned nonfinancial star recognition sold more than twice as many condoms as the other groups on average; financial incentives were not detectably effective in that setting.

**Failure/boundary.** Recognition can itself be controlling, public or exclusionary; stars are not evidence confetti works. A temporary reward can be appropriate for costly exploration or low-interest work; normal pay and customer obligations must remain outside the game. **Expected value:** credible learning feedback with lower reward cost and fewer unintended incentives. **Reviewer check:** ask what each item signals and what it costs; test actionable private feedback alone against the proposed add-on; monitor quality, employee sentiment and nonrewarded participation. Keep celebration optional, quiet and accessible; don't award “coach” based on reciprocal nominations alone.

### H7. Default to personal progress; test peer competition only where it adds value — A for mixed field effects, not for an optimal visibility switch
**Case mapping:** modifies 3, 5 and 11. **Mechanism:** rank sometimes supplies a useful reference, status or competition; it can also signal futility, turn helping into a cost, and shift effort between tasks or partners. A team progress indicator and a prize tournament are distinct interventions.

**Evidence.** Iwan Barankay, “Rank Incentives: Evidence from a Randomized Workplace Experiment” ([author's Wharton abstract](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/profile/barankay/), unpublished/revision, **abstract only**), reports that removing private rank feedback raised sales by 11% in a three-year experiment on 1,754 furniture salespeople; adding benchmarks attenuated the harm, and the response varied by gender. Jordi Blanes i Vidal and Mareike Nossol (2011), “Tournaments Without Prizes: Evidence from Personnel Records,” *Management Science*, 57(10), 1721–1736 ([abstract record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ormnsc/v57y2011i10p1721-1736.html), **abstract only**), found a substantial, durable productivity increase after a firm began revealing relative performance to piece-rate workers in a quasi-experiment. Oriana Bandiera, Iwan Barankay and Imran Rasul (2013), “Team Incentives: Evidence from a Firm Level Experiment,” *Journal of the European Economic Association*, 11(5), 1079–1114 ([author working paper](https://docs.iza.org/dp6279.pdf)), report rankings reduced team productivity 14% relative to piece rates alone and the subsequent cash tournament increased it 24% relative to rankings; negative effects concentrated at the bottom and positive tournament effects at the top. Mitesh S. Patel et al. (2019), “Effectiveness of Behaviorally Designed Gamification Interventions With Social Incentives for Increasing Physical Activity Among Overweight and Obese Adults Across the United States: The STEP UP Randomized Clinical Trial,” *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 179(12), 1624–1632 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2019.3505), **abstract only**), found competition +920 steps/day vs wearable-feedback control during the game and +569 during follow-up (95% CI 142–996); collaboration and support also improved during treatment. These were game bundles in a step trial, not direct tests of named sales leaderboards.

**Failure/boundary.** A single top-five board can make most people irrelevant or expose sensitive small groups. Bracket size does not solve case difficulty or opt-in selection. **Expected value:** target social energy without making cooperation or privacy costly. **Reviewer check:** choose personal baseline and an aggregate shared goal as comparator; if testing rank, make peer display reversible and voluntary, compare genuinely comparable groups, and measure bottom-tail behaviour, opt-outs, helping, quality and selective case choice. More activity from already-strong reps alone does not establish programme value.

### H8. Separate self-feedback, coaching access and surveillance — A for general monitoring risk; D for the exact access policy
**Case mapping:** modifies 5; contradicts 6 as a blanket default. **Mechanism:** seeing one's own progress is useful for self-regulation (H2). Unrestricted employer monitoring has a different power relationship and can cause workers to hide problems, avoid experimentation or respond to the measure instead of improving the work.

**Evidence.** Daniel M. Ravid, Jerod C. White, David L. Tomczak, Ahleah F. Miles and Tara S. Behrend (2023; online 2022), “A Meta-Analysis of the Effects of Electronic Performance Monitoring on Work Outcomes,” *Personnel Psychology*, 76(1), 5–40 ([accepted preprint](https://osf.io/ef43u/download)), found no evidence of a general worker-performance benefit across 94 independent samples and greater stress/strain; less invasive and more transparent monitoring was associated with more favourable attitudes. Ethan S. Bernstein (2012), “The Transparency Paradox: A Role for Privacy in Organizational Learning and Operational Control,” *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 57(2), 181–216 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1177/0001839212453028), **abstract only**), finds a modest increase in group privacy improved line performance in a Chinese factory field experiment and describes costly concealment under observation. David Holman, Claire Chissick and Peter Totterdell (2002), “The Effects of Performance Monitoring on Emotional Labor and Well-Being in Call Centers,” *Motivation and Emotion*, 26, 57–81 ([publisher abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1015194108376), **abstract only**), examine perceived intensity, clarity and developmental purpose in a questionnaire study of 347 reps in two centres; not a causal gamification study.

**Failure/boundary.** Limited individual access can be justified for appeals, security or requested coaching; disclosure alone is not necessity or meaningful consent to public comparison. Hidden cards can still be inferred by subtraction. **Expected value:** more honest exception reporting, lower risk of repurposing trial data for discipline. **Reviewer check:** walk through employee, direct manager, leadership and administrator views; make private progress default; use aggregate-by-default leadership views, purpose-restricted individual access, access logs, deletion dates and explicit prohibition on disciplinary reuse; verify a rep can opt out of public play without losing ordinary work opportunities or credit eligibility.

### H9. Confirm quickly; settle only verified rewards, without clawing back wages — B for some framing effects; D for settlement timing
**Case mapping:** modifies 8 and 13. **Mechanism:** immediate specific acknowledgement closes the learning loop; settlement protects integrity while outcomes mature. A reversal of a provisional signal is a correction, unlike taking back cash employees already possess.

**Evidence.** Edward L. Thorndike (1911), *Animal Intelligence*, ch. 5 ([text](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Thorndike/Animal/chap5.htm)), proposed a law of effect based on consequences strengthening responses to the same situation. Charles B. Ferster and B. F. Skinner (1957), *Schedules of Reinforcement* (Appleton-Century-Crofts; [B. F. Skinner Foundation description](https://www.bfskinner.org/product/schedules-of-reinforcement-pdf/), **summary only**), document that different contingencies produce different response patterns, mostly in pigeon experiments. Fixed/variable ratio (counts), fixed/variable interval (time), and withdrawal/extinction are useful distinctions, not a recommendation for surprise prizes at work. Roland G. Fryer Jr., Steven D. Levitt, John A. List and Sally Sadoff (2012), “Enhancing the Efficacy of Teacher Incentives through Loss Aversion: A Field Experiment,” *NBER Working Paper* 18237 ([paper](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w18237/w18237.pdf)), find math-score effects of 0.201–0.398 SD across loss-treatment specifications; conventional gain effects were smaller and not significant. In a distinct school-level NYC experiment, Roland G. Fryer Jr. (2013), “Teacher Incentives and Student Achievement: Evidence from New York City Public Schools,” *Journal of Labor Economics*, 31(2), 373–407 ([working-paper abstract](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16850), **abstract only**), found no evidence of gains in achievement, attendance or graduation in over 200 schools; this is not a replication of the loss-framed arm. Advance payment plus a repayable bonus is not evidence for punitive worker fines, gift card clawbacks or a specific verification lag. Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini (2000), “A Fine Is a Price,” *Journal of Legal Studies*, 29(1), 1–17 ([paper](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/fine.pdf)), show late pickups rose when day-care centres introduced a small fine and did not fall after removal. It is a warning about changed meaning, not evidence that all penalties increase the behaviour.

**Failure/boundary.** Instant tangible swag that cannot be reversed reintroduces the exploit. Excessively delayed or opaque validation weakens the link to the act; valid exception disputes must not be treated as misconduct. **Expected value:** prompt reinforcement of the right act without paying for invalid events. **Reviewer check:** show a provisional event and reason/status promptly, finalise against the actual distribution of recall latency (not a magic 14 days), specify what happens after cutoff and who resolves disputes. Keep tentative points distinct from earned pay; do not use loss-framing by default.

### H10. Shadow-test the rule and make outcomes replayable — C for instrumentation lessons; D for implementation choice
**Case mapping:** supports 2 and 9 as controls, modifies 12. **Mechanism:** auditable rules, identifiers and immutable event evidence permit independent checks, corrections and disputes. Recomputing from a source of record is useful only if it retains enough history and provenance.

**Evidence.** Rita Marques, João Gregório, Fernando Pinheiro, Pedro Póvoa, Miguel Mira da Silva and Luís Velez Lapão (2017), “How Can Information Systems Provide Support to Nurses’ Hand Hygiene Performance? Using Gamification and Indoor Location to Improve Hand Hygiene Awareness and Reduce Hospital Infections,” *BMC Medical Informatics and Decision Making*, 17, 15 ([article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12911-017-0410-z)), could not test the combined intervention because first and second sensing approaches were too inaccurate or unstable. No hand-hygiene or infection effect was established. Daniela Pires et al. (2021), “Effect of Wearing a Novel Electronic Wearable Device on Hand Hygiene Compliance Among Health Care Workers,” *JAMA Network Open*, 4(2), e2035331 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2020.35331), **abstract only**), show a related proxy hazard: in the stepped-wedge trial, the device raised hand-rub volume and duration, but adjusted compliance did not improve (OR 1.03, 95% CI 0.75–1.42). Neither source compares policy-object architectures or nightly vs intraday calculation.

**Failure/boundary.** Current-state snapshots erase first-send history; an accurate replay can still miss non-tool activity, duplicate credits or encode a biased eligibility rule. A configurable sponsor switch is not permission to change the rules on participants midstream. **Expected value:** catch bad instrumentation before it creates rewards, disputes or apparent lift. **Reviewer check:** independently shadow-score a stratified set of known true/false cases before launching; inspect eligibility timestamps, stable case IDs, duplicate and late records, correction reasons, clock boundaries, permissions and versioned rules; verify the same policy/data version produces the same result; have a stop-and-reconcile procedure, not silent edits.

### H11. Run a small comparison before making the larger programme a policy — A for testing heterogeneous designs; D for the local rollout plan
**Case mapping:** new; modifies 7, 11–13. **Mechanism:** a comparison against workflow improvement and plain feedback estimates what the game adds. A fixed outcome, contemporaneous measurement, defined harm thresholds and post-reward observation separate improvement from concurrent changes and early enthusiasm.

**Evidence.** Milkman et al. (2021), H5, tested 54 programmes in one field setting with a common outcome; many failed to move it detectably, most effects did not remain detectable, and forecasters did not predict the ordering reliably. Steven D. Levitt and John A. List (2011), “Was There Really a Hawthorne Effect at the Hawthorne Plant? An Analysis of the Original Illumination Experiments,” *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics*, 3(1), 224–238 ([working-paper abstract](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15016); **abstract only**), find the traditional story unsupported by the recovered data, with hints of subtler observation effects. Jim McCambridge, John Witton and Diana R. Elbourne (2014), “Systematic Review of the Hawthorne Effect: New Concepts Are Needed to Study Research Participation Effects,” *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*, 67(3), 267–277 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2013.08.015), **abstract only**), found heterogeneous effects in 19 studies and no securely generalisable magnitude. Stefano DellaVigna and Elizabeth Linos (2022), “RCTs to Scale: Comprehensive Evidence From Two Nudge Units,” *Econometrica*, 90(1), 81–116 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA18709), **abstract only**), report 1.4 percentage points of average take-up lift across 126 government RCTs, vs 8.7 points in their academic comparison sample. It illustrates transport and publication risk, not a workplace effect estimate.

**Failure/boundary.** A whole-team launch vs last month confounds seasonality, new management attention and mostly easy renewals; analysing opt-in users alone selects for interest. Spillovers can swamp a within-team comparison. **Expected value:** learn whether to scale, change or remove the programme without necessarily building every mechanic. **Reviewer check:** prerecord eligible opportunities, first-send/quality definitions, decision threshold, cost and safe stop; give both groups basic workflow support and equivalent measurement; randomise by rep if spillovers are small, by team if enough independent teams exist, or use a staggered feasibility pilot with explicit noncausal limits. Report intention-to-treat, denominators, opportunity mix, uncertainty and follow-up; don't turn an underpowered or noisy null into “no effect.”

## What the remaining literature does and does not buy

- **Goodhart/Campbell:** Charles A. E. Goodhart (1984), “Problems of Monetary Management: The UK Experience,” in *Monetary Theory and Practice*, pp. 91–121 ([chapter abstract](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-349-17295-5_4), **abstract only**), describes trouble implementing monetary control after policy changed. Donald T. Campbell (1979), “Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change,” *Evaluation and Program Planning*, 2(1), 67–90 ([authorised reprint](https://jmde.com/index.php/jmde_1/article/download/297/292) of the earlier 1976 manuscript), argues that the greater the use of an indicator for decisions, the more pressure there is to corrupt it and distort the processes it measures; examples include crime statistics and failures to record complaints. Use Kerr/OCC and the proxy trials for inspected examples rather than attributing a quantified effect to either “law.”
- **Crowding and temptation bundling:** Richard M. Ryan and Edward L. Deci (2000), “Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation, Social Development, and Well-Being,” *American Psychologist*, 55(1), 68–78 ([paper](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf)), distinguish autonomy, competence and relatedness; use the framework to ask how an intervention feels, not to declare what will win. Uri Gneezy and Aldo Rustichini (2000), “Pay Enough or Don't Pay at All,” *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 115(3), 791–810 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1162/003355300554917), **abstract only**), report nonmonotonic compensation effects in experiments. Katherine L. Milkman, Julia A. Minson and Kevin G. M. Volpp (2014), “Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym: An Evaluation of Temptation Bundling,” *Management Science*, 60(2), 283–299 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784), **abstract only**), find initial gym visits 51% higher when audiobooks were gym-only and 29% higher with encouragement to restrict them; effects declined, particularly after Thanksgiving. An enjoyable work pairing may help start, but has switching and novelty costs. Giving reps faster useful feedback is a closer analogue than locking an unrelated perk behind compliance.
- **Tournaments and gamification syntheses:** Edward P. Lazear and Sherwin Rosen (1981), “Rank-Order Tournaments as Optimum Labor Contracts,” *Journal of Political Economy*, 89(5), 841–864 ([working-paper abstract](https://www.nber.org/papers/w0401), **abstract only**), show an efficiency equivalence under specified assumptions (including risk neutrality), not that a sales leaderboard beats personal feedback. Juho Hamari, Jonna Koivisto and Harri Sarsa (2014), “Does Gamification Work? A Literature Review of Empirical Studies on Gamification,” *HICSS*, 3025–3034 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/W1998933811), **abstract only**), report mostly promising but context- and user-dependent results. Jonna Koivisto and Juho Hamari (2019), “The Rise of Motivational Information Systems: A Review of Gamification Research,” *International Journal of Information Management*, 45, 191–210 ([abstract record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijinfomgt.2018.10.013), **abstract only**), find many mixed results across a review of 819 studies. Michael Sailer and Lisa Homner (2020), “The Gamification of Learning: A Meta-Analysis,” *Educational Psychology Review*, 32, 77–112 ([full text](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)), estimate g = .49 cognitive, .36 motivational and .25 behavioural outcomes in educational settings; the latter two were less stable in higher-rigor subsets. Do not apply the estimates to an individual workplace mechanic or durability.
- **Badges, peer review and work quality:** Ashton Anderson, Daniel Huttenlocher, Jon Kleinberg and Jure Leskovec (2013), “Steering User Behavior with Badges,” *WWW*, 95–106 ([paper](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/www13-badges.pdf)), show Stack Overflow user actions shift around badge thresholds, consistent with steering effort among actions; not proof of better answers or enduring habit. Yi-Jen Ho, Siyuan Liu and Lei Wang (2023), “Fun Shopping: A Randomized Field Experiment on Gamification,” *Information Systems Research*, 34(2), 766–785 ([institutional abstract](https://scholarsphere.psu.edu/resources/cd190204-d465-495f-8fcc-114dca408e1f), **abstract only**), report sales lift of 21.5% for badges and 22.5% for leaderboards during treatment in an Asian shopping mall, with both still significant in follow-up. This is a positive counterexample to “games always wear off,” not a workplace estimate. Kohei Yoshigami, Taishi Hayashi, Masateru Tsunoda, Hidetake Uwano, Shunichiro Sasaki and Kenichi Matsumoto (2019), “How Does Time Conscious Rule of Gamification Affect Coding and Review?” *IEICE Transactions on Information and Systems*, E102.D(12), 2435–2438 ([paper](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/transinf/E102.D/12/E102.D_2019MPL0002/_pdf)), report a small code/review experiment: a speed-framed score's review-time ratio difference was not significant (p = .12); they flag the trade-off with fault detection. Neither is a reason to reward PR counts. A preprint in school practice illustrates the difference between starting and repeating: Raphaëlle Aulagnon, Julián Cristia, Santiago Cueto and Ofer Malamud, “Streaks to Success? The Effects of Highlighting Streaks on Student Effort and Learning” (*NBER Working Paper* 34173, 2025; [paper](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34173/w34173.pdf)), reports personalised reminders drew more students in whereas streaks helped the intensive margin; the endline test subset was about 1,500 of approximately 60,000 randomised, and differences from the other treatment arms were not significant.
- **Call centres and consequential workflow:** Holman et al. (H8) concerns monitoring, not points. Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey R. Raymond, “Generative AI at Work” (*NBER Working Paper* 31161, 2023; [working-paper abstract](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161), **abstract only**), report roughly 14% more customer-support issues resolved per hour after staggered assistant rollout, with a larger gain for novice/low-skilled workers. It is evidence that the **underlying tool** can matter and that gains vary, not an estimate of the incentive effect. [Centrical’s Comdata case study](https://centrical.com/case-studies/comdata-case-study/) (vendor case, D) describes competition alongside microlearning and performance management and reports 12% more sales per hour; it does not establish what the competition added, what happened to call quality, or whether lift persisted. No controlled call-centre game estimate was used for a reward rule.

| 1 | **Modify** | Keep one real unit, deduplicated. Specify whether credit and the target use the same first customer send or intentionally use separate definitions, and why. Validate quality separately; avoid a gate that rewards rushing or hiding cases (H1). |
| 2 | **Keep** | A single source for versioned, published rules and decisions is sensible governance, not an experimentally validated motivator. Freeze eligibility/thresholds for a cohort; test late facts and disclose any prospective changes (H10, D). |

| 4 | **Modify** | Prefer an eligible-work cue and gentle re-entry. Preserve the safe manual route and freeze no-opportunity periods, keep “excused” separate from completed, and strip identifying details from blocker reports. A return can matter more than calendar continuity (H3/H5). |
| 5 | **Modify** | No named peer board by default. If tested, require meaningful opt-in, easy reversal, privacy thresholds and comparable cohorts; don't offer mandatory exposure as an equivalent sponsor setting. Top-five-only has no evidence-backed default (H7/H8). |

Early exit needs a predeclared safety/feasibility rule or independent verification and still requires post-exit observation (H3/H4/H11). |

Remove range unless it corresponds to appropriate proficiency; validate helping and avoid punishing dependably narrow case mixes. ## Blind design rubric — 10 criteria, 0–10 each

Score the design the agent actually specified, not whether it recites the literature or resembles Rally. No bonus for adding prizes, ranks, streaks or badges; omitting one can earn full credit if the underlying goal and risk are addressed. Use integers: 0 = absent or contrary, 5 = useful but missing a consequential safeguard/test, 10 = decision-ready at the scenario's scale; use intermediate numbers between anchors. Sum to 100 and record a short reason per criterion. A design can score well yet be **not launchable**: flag unmitigated customer/safety risk, invalid core measurement, or coercive employer use separately; these are not cancelled by a high aggregate score. Freeze this rubric and judge instructions before evaluating outputs.

| Criterion | 0 | 5 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Behaviour and value alignment | Counts logins, drafts or game actions only. | Names a real act, but target, credit and outcome are misaligned or underspecified. | Names who does what, on which eligible opportunity and when; aligns success/credit/quality or explicitly reconciles different measures. |
| 2. Workflow feasibility | Assumes motivation is the bottleneck. | Lists barriers or training, but no baseline diagnosis or exception ownership. | Checks real access and friction, demonstrates expected value net of QA, embeds a work cue and safe fallback, and names a blocker owner. |
| 3. Opportunity-adjusted goals and feedback | Raw contest volume, no usable feedback. | Normalised personal or team goals, but weak handling of small books, case mix or next action. | Credible goals tied to baseline/feasibility, clear n/N and next action, with zero opportunities, uneven volume and case mix handled. |
| 4. Quality and anti-gaming | Pays for an easily gamed proxy; no guardrails. | One quality check, but little detection of displacement or no owner/trigger. | Adversarial examples, relevant independent quality and displacement checks, proportionate audit and concrete pause/recovery rule. |
| 5. Incentive and feedback choice | High stakes/use of losses or decorative mechanics without a testable rationale. | Proportionate rewards, but unclear value signal, fairness, timing or verification. | Specific timely feedback; optional additions tied to a mechanism and net value; explains settlement, exceptions and why stakes fit. |
| 6. Lapses and safe re-entry | Punishes leave/outages or creates all-or-nothing failure. | Can restart informally; treats missed, excused and completed events ambiguously. | Clear differentiated rules and an easy next opportunity after a miss, without pressure to log low-quality work or induce misses; streak optional. |
| 7. Privacy and social effects | Mandatory named surveillance or shaming, no meaningful limits. | Discloses visibility or groups peers but purpose, power or harm not addressed. | Purpose-limited views, optional peer display if any, protection against small-group inference, and checks for morale, exclusion and cooperation. |
| 8. Operability and audit | Undefined eligibility/source; no appeal. | A source of record but weak event history or correction rules. | Replayable event/policy versions, independent shadow validation, permissions, transparent corrections and staffed appeals. |
| 9. Durability and exit | Declares success from in-game numbers or assumes a fixed habit clock. | Ends prizes or tracks a vague follow-up. | Plans withdrawal and retained workflow support, measures subsequent eligible opportunities and quality after payout, and defines persistence/stop rules. |
| 10. Learning design | Single before/after claim or cherry-picked enthusiasts. | Credible baseline plus a comparison or operational success criterion, but with gaps. | Isolates incremental value over plain support, handles spillovers/case mix and uncertainty, and predefines outcomes, costs, harms and decisions at feasible sample size. |

**Lowest-cost rollout:** shadow-score representative cases, observe a few live workflows and usability-test the private feedback first. Compare plain feedback to at most one extra mechanic in the initial pilot. Require a separate justification before engineering a ranking service, elaborate rewards or management reporting.

For calibration, have both blind judges score the same non-held-out example before freezing instructions. Resolve differences in **anchor interpretation**, not by changing the rubric after seeing baseline or skill scores. Keep design-eval variance distinct from estimated employee outcomes. For the weakest levers (celebration, complex badge menus, exact thresholds), prefer deletion unless the proposed test isolates meaningful added value.
